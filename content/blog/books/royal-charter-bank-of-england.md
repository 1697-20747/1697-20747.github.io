The Bank of England was constituted by Royal Charter granted by William III in 1694. The Charter emerged from a straightforward transaction — a syndicate of merchants led by William Paterson agreed to lend £1.2 million to a cash-strapped Crown at 8% interest, and in return received the right to incorporate as a bank with the power to issue notes, hold deposits, and extend credit.

What made it revolutionary was not the banking itself but the constitutional arrangement underneath it — the loan was permanent, converting a one-off debt into an enduring obligation of the state. Parliament guaranteed repayment, binding future governments to the commitments of the present one. This created the world's first funded national debt and established the principle that sovereign borrowing could extend across generations. The Charter is the founding document of modern public finance as much as it is of central banking.

## Observations

So many gems in here. The Bank was founded because Charles II didn't repay the debts he owed to goldsmiths. The bill comes due. What was it supposed to do? Fund the war with France of course. Yes, that was a prima facie objective of the Bank of England — it says so right there on the page. Euro sceptics indeed.

How much is that in today's money? Using the Bank of England inflation calculator, £1.2 million in 1694 equates to approximately £203,967,498.90 today. Not that much really, is it? We can probably skip the 90p. That is an increase — or better, a decrease in value — of 16,897.3%. At an average of 1.56%. So in a way, if you want to cheat and play the long game, the Bank of England has actually met its inflation target. But that rocky path to perdition is a longer story for another day.
