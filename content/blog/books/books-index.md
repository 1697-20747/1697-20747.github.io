## Military History & Leadership

**Once an Eagle** — Anton Myrer
: A sweeping military novel following two officers across decades of American wars. One of the finest studies of leadership, character, and moral compromise ever written. Required reading at several US war colleges for good reason.

*Key Observations:*
If you have ever been in the army, or have suffered the usual tyranny in a large financial institution (yes large Banks, I do mean you), this one is for you. This is a military classic, that transcends the domain into life. Are you Sam? Really? There are no untainted hero's here. This is the life lesson. The title comes from the fable: "So in the Libyan fable it is told / That once an eagle, stricken with a dart, / Said, when he saw the fashion of the shaft, / 'With our own feathers, not by others' hands, / Are we now smitten'". Read this book for a good history of war. Re read it as many times as you need to until you understand the enemy is you. All of our carts bounce around in the rutted tracks of our deep rooted tendencies that we laid down long ago to get where we are.

**A concise history of warfare Field-Marshal Viscount Montgomery of Alamein.** — Field Marshal Montgomery
: Montgomery's own account of warfare from ancient times to the mid-twentieth century, written with the authority of a man who commanded armies across North Africa and Northwest Europe. Clear-eyed on strategy, leadership, and the recurring mistakes that commanders make across centuries.

*Key Observations:*
A nice summary of key battles, with a good tactical vs strategy examination. Key observation in literal sense from this book is that 'Tactics, strategy and resources need to be in alignment to attain success'. Everyone who ever worked on a large project should read this as many times as it takes to be understood. You cannot escape the contraint of resources. How many times in your life have you seen things fail because the resources (people, capital, time, technology) were materially diminished compared to the ask? Failure in such situation is the fault of the person setting the strategy, not that of those executing. Strategy at the end of the day is the articulation of tbe bridge from means, to the end. It reflects the painful, but necessary, choices made in the face of constraint. There are always constraints.

**The U.S. Army Leadership Field Manual (FM 22-100)** — Department of the Army
: The official leadership doctrine used in every U.S. Army officer training programme for over fifty years. Built around the core principle of "Be, Know, Do" — character first, then competence, then action — it covers leadership at every level from squad to strategic command, illustrated with historical vignettes from Marshall to Schwarzkopf. Surprisingly applicable well beyond the military. 

*Key Observations:*
This one you can read one hundred times. Mac graduated from West Point in 1966. He was a two-tour Vietnam veteran, first as a Lieutenant and again as a Captain. As a LT Colonel teaching at Ft. Leavenworth, during what would ultimately be his final assignment, he rewrote the leadership manual, FM 22-100, for the United States Army. This was based in large part on lessons from Vietnam, bitter one's at that. Harris died on October 13, 1983, the same month the Army published the revised FM 22-100. This book is moral as it is historical. This one of the most famous texts on leadership in the West. In 1985, West Point established the Boyd M. Harris Leadership Award, and in 2016 Mac was inducted into the Hall of Fame at Fort Leavenworth, KS. A trained psychologist this is text on human beings under pressure. It is a shame that he never lived longer to know that this book became legend. This text was a slap in the face to the Army post Vietnam, no one wanted to know about this. It was a gift to future generations, as well as to us. Written by someone who was there, at the pointy end, and saw what failures looked like. Can you live up to this standard? Sure about that?

The commander of a unit is responsible for all that his unit does, or fails to do. Not often in the corporate world that you see anyone not slink away from the latter.

**Lombard Street** — Walter Bagehot
: The foundational text on central banking. Written in 1873, Bagehot explains how the Bank of England should act as lender of last resort during financial panics — lend freely, at high rates, against good collateral. Every financial crisis since has been measured against whether policymakers followed his prescription.

*Key Observations:*
The classic must read if you ever study central banking. Or work in one. Go on, give it a go.

**The Royal Charter of the Bank of England** *(1694)*
: The Bank of England was constituted by Royal Charter granted by William III in 1694. The Charter emerged from a straightforward transaction — a syndicate of merchants led by William Paterson agreed to lend £1.2 million to a cash-strapped Crown at 8% interest, and in return received the right to incorporate as a bank with the power to issue notes, hold deposits, and extend credit. What made it revolutionary was not the banking itself but the constitutional arrangement underneath it — the loan was permanent, converting a one-off debt into an enduring obligation of the state. Parliament guaranteed repayment, binding future governments to the commitments of the present one. This created the world's first funded national debt and established the principle that sovereign borrowing could extend across generations. The Charter is the founding document of modern public finance as much as it is of central banking.
