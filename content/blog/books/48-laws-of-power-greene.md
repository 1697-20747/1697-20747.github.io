Greene compresses centuries of court intrigue, military history, and corporate backstabbing into 48 rules for accumulating and keeping power. Each law gets its own chapter, its own cast of historical figures, and its own moral: never outshine the master, conceal your intentions, win through actions not argument. It reads less like a study and more like a very long, very entertaining briefing from a cynical advisor.

## Observations

The book works as a catalogue of behaviour you will recognise from any office, court, or family. Greene is a genuinely good storyteller, and the historical vignettes — Talleyrand, Sun Tzu, P.T. Barnum, assorted Medicis — are the real draw. You come away with a sharper eye for the people around you who are quietly playing a status game you didn't know was on.

The scholarship underneath it does not hold up to much pressure, though. Each "law" is supported by two or three handpicked anecdotes, never by anything resembling a systematic survey of cases where the same behaviour failed. There is no engagement with counter-examples, no citation trail back to primary sources for most of the stories, and no acknowledgment that history is long enough to find an anecdote to support almost any rule you'd care to invent. It is survivorship bias dressed up as a system — closer to a well-curated commonplace book than a tested theory of power. Treat it as a collection of provocative case studies, not as research. 

Let us take historical battles as a proxy for what in truth were complex situations. Battles turn on many things, weather, food, mood, leadership, technology, etc. Julius Caesar defeated Pompey at Battle of Pharsalus, outmanned at the time. Read the Commentarii de Bello Civili. Did Julius Caesar really win over Pompey because he instructed his army to push spear points into the faces of some pretty boys? It does not mean it didn't happen, it probably did. It was probably an intelligent tactic at the time. It might have been driven by an intent to maintain possession of the pila as a stabbing weapon. A pila is the classic Roman javelin, weighted at the shaft, with a penetrating head on a softer iron shaft designed to bend to prevent re use. Instead of thrown in a one and done manoeuvre as against heavy infantry, retaining them (not throwing) for repeated attack on cavalry might have been solid battlefield tactics. None of us where there, including Plutarch. Caesar was a noted propagandist. The point here is over simplification makes for a nice neat vignette to support the pre selected case. I personally think Caeser was a great tactician. Possibly also lucky at times, but surely skilled. Unlike the pathetic lot we have today, he fought his own wars from the front line and did the wet work at great personal risk. Respect.

So then, worth a read?

- Worth reading for the stories and the discomfort it produces. Its a good whip through history.
- It lays bare human behaviors and motivation how it really is. If you don't realise this.......
- Worth resisting as a manual. Don't be a tyrant, there is more than enough to go around already. Live your life with some grace. Be a nice person. Don't be a fool either and get abused.
- Its true value lies in its observations. If you are early in your life or career, and think everyone is nice like your parents taught you to be, then this book is for you. The world is full to the brim of people who will screw you over, politics is a brutalist sport. This book can forewarn you of behaviours that you will surely see, as well as be victim to.
