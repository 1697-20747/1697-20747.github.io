## Overview

This one is a biggie. The inception of this is a project to use AI (whatever that means) to write credit papers. Easier said than done. My understanding is that project teams trying this is straight LLM models got stuck in a rut. Which is not surprising.

So the intent here is to create a credit paper from data. Why is a bit of a moot point, as personally I don't think its the right objective. Its a bit like precision engineering Fred Flintsone's car. Why? We have all moved on. But needs must. It also presumes only two other aspects. Three really I suppose:
1. There is a financial statement available to be downloaded for the relevant entity. This is presumed to be be in pdf form. Again why we use this is beyond me, but there you go.
2. There will be a statutory Pillar £3 report available. pdf or excel. Both pointless but it what it is.
3. The output will be a credit paper. Again, not really the right outcome, but this is what people want apparently.
4. If you can't guess, this is for asset class of Financial Institutions.

Every scrap of open source data possible will be used. The basic intent here is to use a broad swathe of data, python and open source libraries for tooling, and then a bespoke post trained mid size LLM with open weights. The latter is the key, this is to be a stand alone piece of software that runs offline once trained. I have also tried to be generic in the structure, so I can switch this across asset classes, eg Project Finance. Key challenges:
- I don't know how to do this, even as I am decent on machine learning and agents, never applied this to LLM before.
- Open data is key, more is better.
- Hallucinations are to be minimized. I mean every token generated by an LLM is a hallucination when you get into transformer architecture, but this is be designed to be minimal.
- I am compute constrained, so training will be capped. Co labs and a mac mini with 16gb are my only tools. This is not nearly enough. But the package is designed to scale afterwards.
- I hate post production with a passion, so there is no fancy formatting. Its going to be text based, with md as well. As an act of rebellion.
- The working version is going to go on github. If you know what you are doing this will be enough. If you don't, bad luck. I am not spoon-feeding you.
- The advanced version staying offline, as I intend to continuously refine this.
- Every number in a credit paper will be compared to population, eg CET 1 is 12.6% [this falls into the 3rd decline amongst the overall population]. Nothing stand alone here, comparative analysis is baked into the design from day one.
- BYO credit papers. The training model has a folder where you can add your own credit papers to build out the training.
- Everything is open sourced, nothing stolen.
- Ratings principles are adhered to.
- CAMELS is the over arching approach.

The key part for me here is the task. The language around credit analysis is specific and nuanced. But once you know it its not that hard. Hence a trained LLM is best, we don't need GAI models for this task. But it is a very specific domain. This screams smaller model. I might actually pre train a focused model on this if I can get the compute at some point.

Beyond this, every tool I know and some I don't were used. Every single data source at scale was used. They are all documented.

## The Data Problem

The first thing you realise when building a domain-specific LLM is that data is everything. Generic models trained on internet text know the word "CET1" but have no idea what 14.0% means relative to a population of banks, what the regulatory minimum is, or how Fitch would frame it in a rating rationale. You need domain data and lots of it.

The pipeline ultimately pulls from five separate source streams:

**Stream 1 — Annual Reports.** SEC EDGAR has every 10-K filing for every listed US bank going back decades, in machine-readable HTML. The downloader pulls these automatically by CIK number. UK and EU banks are messier — IR pages, different formats, occasional OCR required for scanned PDFs. Fitch's own rating methodology document is a scanned PDF. 65 pages of it. So we OCR it at 300 DPI and chunk it. This is not elegant but it works.

**Stream 2 — EBA Transparency Exercise.** The European Banking Authority publishes detailed financial data for 120+ EU banks every six months. Capital ratios, NPL ratios, profitability, liquidity — standardised across all institutions. 156 banks, 2019 to 2025, Q2 and Q4 periods. This is gold for benchmarking because every bank is on the same basis. 1,463 training pairs from one CSV file.

**Stream 3 — FDIC Call Reports.** Every US bank files quarterly with the FDIC. They have a public REST API. Top 100 banks by assets, annual periods, 5 years back. Tier 1 ratio, net charge-off rate, ROA, NIM, efficiency ratio, loan-to-deposit ratio — all confirmed field names from the live API because the documentation lies and you have to debug it yourself. 492 pairs.

**Stream 4 — Rating Agency Methodology.** Fitch, S&P, DBRS Morningstar all publish their bank rating methodologies publicly (Fitch and S&P require free registration). Basel Committee, OCC Comptroller's Handbook, FDIC examination manuals, EBA SREP guidelines, PRA supervisory statements — all free, all downloaded automatically. These teach the model the analytical language and regulatory thresholds.

**Stream 5 — Your own credit papers.** Drop DOCX files into `credit_reports/` and they become gold-standard training examples. The model learns from real analyst prose, not templated output. These are weighted 2x in training.

Total: **3,832 training pairs** from 123 source documents across the US, UK and EU.

## The Training Pair Quality Problem

Raw extraction from annual reports gives you text and numbers but the LLM needs to see what good analyst output looks like. The naive approach is to template the responses — fill in the metrics and write boilerplate. This teaches the model structure but not judgment.

The solution is a one-time quality upgrade pass using the Claude API (Haiku model, cheapest tier). Each of the 854 financial statement pairs gets its template response replaced with a proper analyst-written analysis: structured assessment, regulatory context, peer comparison, rating agency framing, key risks. Cost: under $5 for the full run. The script is fully resumable — it saves progress after every single API call, so if it hits rate limits or you kill it, it picks up exactly where it stopped.

The result is three quality tiers in the training data:

- **Gold** — real analyst credit papers you provide (DOCX, 2x weighted)
- **Upgraded** — financial statement pairs with Claude Haiku analyst prose
- **Structured** — EBA and FDIC data with rule-based assessments and peer context
- **Extracted** — rating agency methodology chunked directly from source documents

## The Benchmarking Problem

A credit paper that says "CET1 ratio is 14.0%" is useless without context. Is that strong? Weak? How does it compare to peers? This was a design requirement from day one — every number needs population context.

The benchmark index is built from all processed financial data. For each metric it computes full distribution statistics: mean, median, p10, p25, p75, p90, and decile thresholds. Separate distributions for all years and recent years (last 3). Regional breakdowns for US, UK, EU, AU where sample size permits.

At inference time, every extracted metric gets its decile appended before the LLM sees it:

```
CET1 RATIO: 14.0% [Source: 2025-lbg-annual-report.pdf, p.53]
  ← 7th decile globally (median: 13.8%, p10: 10.5%, p90: 14.2%; n=847 bank-years)
```

The LLM then incorporates this into its analysis rather than making up peer comparisons. The benchmark table appears at the top of every generated credit paper:

| Metric | Value | Global Decile | Median | p10–p90 |
|--------|-------|---------------|--------|---------|
| CET1 Ratio | 14.0% [p.53] | **7th** | 13.8% | 10.5–14.2% |
| Leverage Ratio | 5.4% [p.53] | **5th** | 5.5% | 5.1–9.3% |
| LCR | 145% [p.183] | **4th** | 165% | 150–171% |

The benchmark gets better as more data is added. With FDIC and EBA included it covers 800+ bank-years across three regions.

## The Compute Problem

16GB of unified memory on an M2 Pro. This is the constraint everything else bends around.

The base Qwen2.5-7B model at full precision loads at 15GB. There is essentially nothing left for training. The solution is to use the pre-quantised 4-bit version from the MLX community hub — 4GB loaded, leaving 12GB for activations and gradients. Even then, sequence length has to be 512 tokens to avoid Metal GPU OOM errors. The full-precision model tried twice and crashed the system both times. The 4-bit version with 512 tokens and gradient checkpointing runs fine — peak memory 5.7GB, stable throughout.

Training speed: 0.5 iterations per second. 400–600 iterations: about 20–30 minutes. Validation loss: 3.14 → 0.876 across three Mac training runs.

The MLX API changes between versions caught us. mlx-lm 0.31.3 uses `python -m mlx_lm lora` (subcommand style, not module style). `--lora-layers` was renamed `--num-layers`. LoRA rank and alpha moved to a YAML config file. The training script detects the version at runtime and adapts.

The post-training GGUF export for Ollama fails with a U32 data type error — known MLX/Ollama incompatibility. The workaround is the MLX direct server on port 8080, which exposes an OpenAI-compatible API. This works for inference but crashes the system if you try to run it alongside other applications. 16GB is genuinely not enough for production inference with a 7B model at full precision.

## What It Actually Does

The pipeline from PDF to credit paper:

1. **Triage** — diagnose extraction strategy per file (text PDF, scanned PDF, HTML)
2. **Extract** — PyMuPDF for text, pdfplumber for tables, custom HTML parser for EDGAR filings, tesseract OCR for scanned docs
3. **Metric extraction** — regex patterns for 14 key ratios, each tagged with source page number
4. **Section classification** — keyword matching to CAMELS taxonomy (capital adequacy, asset quality, management, earnings, liquidity, sensitivity) plus Pillar 3 specific sections (RWA breakdown, IRB models, CCR)
5. **Benchmark lookup** — each extracted metric gets its global decile and regional peer decile
6. **LLM analysis** — one call per CAMELS pillar, each prompt includes extracted text, metrics with benchmark context, and Pillar 3 content where available
7. **Assembly** — benchmark table + 6 pillar analyses + audit JSON mapping every claim to source page

The Lloyds 2025 run extracted CET1 14.0% from p.53, ROTE 22.1% from p.105, LCR 145% from p.183, NSFR 124% from p.183. All cited correctly. The Pillar 3 PDF added 133 additional pages of RWA and IRB data including the structural hedge size (£244bn) and IAS 19 pension surplus (£2.6bn).

## How Post-Training of Qwen Actually Works

This section is for anyone who hasn't done this before. It took me longer to figure out than it should have, so documenting it properly.

**The base model.** Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is a 7-billion parameter language model trained by Alibaba on a large general corpus. It already knows how to follow instructions, write structured text, and reason. It knows the word "CET1". What it doesn't know is what a good CAMELS analysis looks like, what the right regulatory thresholds are, when to cite page numbers, or when to refuse to fabricate data. That's what post-training fixes.

**What HuggingFace is.** HuggingFace is essentially a model registry — a place to download model weights. The Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model is published there by Alibaba under an Apache 2.0 licence. Free, open weights, no approval required. You download it once (~15GB for the full precision version, ~4GB for the 4-bit quantised version) and it lives in `~/.cache/huggingface/hub`. You need a free HuggingFace account to download, and an access token from `huggingface.co/settings/tokens`. That's it — it's not a service, it's a file download.

The `mlx-community` organisation on HuggingFace publishes pre-quantised versions of popular models optimised for Apple Silicon. `mlx-community/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-4bit` is the same Qwen model quantised to 4-bit precision, loading in ~4GB instead of ~15GB. This is what makes training feasible on 16GB Mac.

**What QLoRA is.** QLoRA (Quantised Low-Rank Adaptation) is the technique that makes fine-tuning a 7B model feasible on consumer hardware. Instead of updating all 7.6 billion parameters during training — which would require 30–60GB of GPU memory — you freeze the base model weights and only train small "adapter" matrices injected into specific layers. The adapters for this project have 1.4M trainable parameters out of 7.6B total. That's 0.019%. The base model stays frozen and quantised (4-bit, ~4GB). The adapters are tiny — the final `adapters.safetensors` file is about 7MB.

During training, each batch goes through the frozen quantised base model (fast, low memory) plus the small adapter matrices (the only thing being updated). Gradients only flow through the adapters. This is why peak memory stays at 5.7GB throughout training.

**The training loop.** Each iteration takes one batch of training pairs (batch size 1 with gradient accumulation 16 = effective batch of 16). The pair contains a system prompt, a user message (the task + extracted financial data), and an assistant response (the target output). The model predicts each token of the assistant response given all previous context. The loss is cross-entropy between predicted and actual tokens. Gradients update the adapter weights. Repeat 600 times.

Validation loss is computed on the held-out eval set every 25 iterations. It measures how well the model predicts tokens in examples it hasn't been trained on. Starting at 3.14 (barely better than random for this domain), ending at 0.876 (the model has genuinely learned the domain). The absolute number is less important than the trajectory — steady decline without plateau or divergence.

**What MLX is.** MLX is Apple's machine learning framework for Apple Silicon. It uses the unified memory architecture of M-series chips — there's no separate GPU memory, CPU and GPU share the same pool. This is why a 16GB Mac can train at all. On a conventional machine you'd need 16GB of VRAM just for the 4-bit model, separate from system RAM. MLX lets the model and the activations share the same 16GB.

The MLX training script (`mlx_lm.lora`) handles the LoRA training loop. The version we use (0.31.3) changed its API significantly from earlier versions — the arguments `--lora-layers` and `--lora-rank` were removed and moved to a YAML config file. The invocation style changed from `python -m mlx_lm.lora` to `python -m mlx_lm lora`. The training script auto-detects the version and adapts.

**Post-training: fuse and deploy.** After training, the adapter is merged back into the base model weights (`mlx_lm fuse --dequantize`), producing a full-precision fused model. This is then re-quantised to 4-bit for deployment (`mlx_lm convert -q --q-bits 4`). The result is a self-contained model that can be served via Ollama or the MLX direct server.

The GGUF conversion for Ollama fails with a U32 data type error — a known incompatibility between MLX's quantisation format and what Ollama's llama.cpp backend expects. The workaround is to serve via `mlx_lm.server` directly, which exposes an OpenAI-compatible API. The downside is it requires ~15GB to load (full precision fused model) which crashes a 16GB system under load. This is a deployment problem, not a training problem. The solution is Colab Pro to produce a proper GGUF via Unsloth, which does the conversion cleanly.

**Why Qwen2.5 specifically.** Several reasons: Apache 2.0 licence (can be used commercially, no restrictions), strong instruction-following baseline, good multilingual capability (useful for EU bank documents in French/German), competitive with much larger models on structured tasks, actively maintained by Alibaba with regular releases. The 7B size is the sweet spot for this use case — large enough for nuanced domain analysis, small enough to train and run on consumer hardware.

The remaining weakness is fabricated peer comparisons and invented rating agency citations, which come from the base model's tendency to be helpful. These require longer sequence training (2,048+ tokens) to fix properly, as the refusal examples in the training data get truncated at 512 tokens.

## What More Compute Would Actually Change

The current model runs on 16GB of unified memory with 512-token sequence length. The output is structurally correct and cites real page numbers. But there are three specific weaknesses that more compute fixes directly — not incrementally, but categorically.

**Fabricated peer comparisons.** Right now the model invents figures like "HSBC reported CET1 of 13.5%". It does this because the training pairs at 512 tokens are truncated before they reach the peer context section, so the model never sees enough examples of the correct behaviour — which is to write "Peer comparison data not available in the provided source material." At 2,048 tokens the full training pair reaches that section. Ten or twenty well-trained examples of correct refusal behaviour is enough to suppress the fabrication. At 512 tokens those examples are truncated out of training entirely.

**Rating agency citations.** Same problem. The model generates `[Source: Moody's 2025 Credit Report, p.13]` for figures it made up. At 2,048 tokens, the training pairs that teach correct rating agency commentary — which cites methodology frameworks rather than invented reports — are complete. The fine-tuning takes hold. At 512 tokens, that section of every training pair is truncated.

**Analytical depth.** A CET1 analysis at 512 tokens covers the ratio, the regulatory minimum, and a basic assessment. At 4,096 tokens it covers the ratio, all buffer components, the Basel IV output floor trajectory, RWA composition from Pillar 3, IRB model PD/LGD implications, headroom under the MDA threshold, and the rating agency capital scoring methodology. Not because the model is smarter — the base model hasn't changed — but because the training examples it has seen are complete rather than truncated.

**Concretely: what's achievable with an A100 40GB for $10.**

Colab Pro A100 (40GB VRAM), Unsloth 4-bit quantisation, 4,096 tokens, 3 epochs, ~20 minutes. The full training pair for a capital adequacy section — system prompt, extracted annual report text with page references, benchmark decile context, and a complete analyst response with peer comparison refusals, regulatory threshold analysis, and rating agency methodology framing — fits in context entirely. No truncation. The model trains on the complete example.

The output quality difference is not subtle. It's the difference between a model that produces the right structure with some hallucinated details and a model that produces correctly grounded analysis with appropriate refusals where data is absent. For a credit analysis tool, that distinction matters.

**The 14B model question.** Qwen2.5-14B at 4,096 tokens on an A100 adds another dimension — more parameters means better reasoning over complex regulatory text, more nuanced understanding of the interplay between IFRS 9 staging and capital requirements, better calibration of rating agency language. Whether the jump from 7B to 14B is worth the additional compute depends on use case. For internal analytical tooling, 7B at 4,096 tokens is probably sufficient. For anything externally published or client-facing, 14B is worth it.

**Pre-training a domain-specific model** is the next level entirely — train from scratch on a corpus of nothing but banking regulation, annual reports, rating methodologies, and analyst research. This would produce a model that doesn't need to unlearn generic internet text patterns at all. The base knowledge would be pure domain. Requires orders of magnitude more compute and data than fine-tuning but produces a fundamentally different kind of model — one that thinks in Basel III natively rather than having Basel III grafted onto a general-purpose model. Interesting future direction. Not a weekend project.

## Key Takeaways

- Fine-tuning a 7B model on domain-specific data produces meaningfully better output than prompting a generic model, even with a strong system prompt
- The benchmarking requirement (population percentiles for every metric) is non-trivial to implement but essential for the output to be analytically useful
- 16GB unified memory is genuinely insufficient for production inference with a 7B model — it is sufficient for training if you use 4-bit quantisation and short sequences
- The data pipeline is the majority of the work. The model training is the easy part
- QLoRA means you only train 0.019% of parameters — the rest stays frozen. The adapter is 7MB. The base model is 4GB. This is the entire reason consumer hardware training is possible
- HuggingFace is a file download service, not a cloud AI provider. The model runs entirely locally once downloaded
- Sequence length is the single most important training hyperparameter for output quality on this task — more than model size, more than dataset size
- Open source regulatory data (Basel BIS papers, OCC handbooks, FDIC manuals) is underutilised as LLM training material
- Anti-hallucination is an architectural problem, not a prompting problem. Grounded prompting + refusal training + citation enforcement + post-generation validation is the stack
