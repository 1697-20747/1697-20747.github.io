## Overview

This one is a biggie. The inception of this is a project to use AI (whatever that means) to write credit papers. Easier said than done. My understanding is that project teams trying this with straight LLM models got stuck in a rut. Which is not surprising.

So the intent here is to create a credit paper from data. Why is a bit of a moot point, as personally I don't think its the right objective. Its a bit like precision engineering Fred Flintstone's car. Why? We have all moved on. But needs must. It also presumes only a few other aspects:

1. There is a financial statement available to be downloaded for the relevant entity. Presumed to be PDF. Again, why we use this is beyond me, but there you go.
2. There will be a statutory Pillar 3 report available. PDF or Excel. Both pointless formats for data but it is what it is.
3. The output will be a credit paper. Again, not really the right outcome, but this is what people want apparently.
4. If you can't guess, this is for the asset class of Financial Institutions.

Every scrap of open source data possible will be used. The basic intent here is to use a broad swathe of data, Python and open source libraries for tooling, and then a bespoke post-trained mid-size LLM with open weights. The latter is the key — this is to be a standalone piece of software that runs entirely offline once trained. Key challenges:

- I don't know how to do this, even as I am decent on machine learning and agents, never applied this to LLMs before.
- Open data is key, more is better.
- Hallucinations are to be minimised. I mean every token generated by an LLM is a hallucination when you get into transformer architecture, but this is designed to be minimal.
- I am compute constrained. Colab Pro and a Mac mini with 16GB are my only tools. Not nearly enough. But the package is designed to scale afterwards.
- I hate post-production with a passion, so no fancy formatting. Text-based with Markdown. As an act of rebellion.
- The working version goes on GitHub. If you know what you are doing, the README is sufficient. If you don't, bad luck.
- The advanced version stays offline, as I intend to continuously refine it.
- Every number in a credit paper will be compared to population: CET1 is 12.6% — this falls into the 3rd decile amongst the overall population. Comparative analysis is baked into the design from day one.
- BYO credit papers. The training model has a folder where you can add your own analyst papers to build out the training.
- Everything is open sourced, nothing stolen.
- Ratings principles are adhered to.
- CAMELS is the overarching analytical approach.

The language around credit analysis is specific and nuanced. But once you know it, it is not that hard. Hence a trained LLM is best — we don't need GAI models for this specific task. This screams smaller, focused model.

## The Data Problem

The first thing you realise when building a domain-specific LLM is that data is everything. Generic models trained on internet text know the word "CET1" but have no idea what 14.0% means relative to a population of banks, what the regulatory minimum is, or how Fitch would frame it in a rating rationale. You need domain data and lots of it.

The pipeline ultimately pulls from six source streams:

**Stream 1 — Annual Reports.** SEC EDGAR has every 10-K filing for every listed US bank going back decades, in machine-readable HTML. The downloader pulls these automatically by CIK number. UK, EU, Australian and Canadian banks are messier — IR pages, inconsistent formats, date-stamped filenames that change every year, some banks blocking automated downloads entirely. 130+ annual reports currently in the dataset covering Lloyds, Barclays, HSBC, NatWest, Deutsche Bank, Santander, UniCredit, RBC, TD Bank, Scotiabank, ANZ, Westpac, NAB and the top US banks via EDGAR.

**Stream 2 — Pillar 3 Reports.** The richest source of regulatory capital data. Published alongside annual reports but inconsistently — some PDF only, some Excel only, some both. The Excel files vary enormously in structure, column layout, and unit conventions. PDF extraction is imprecise by nature. This is a genuine industry data quality problem. See the README for an extended rant on the subject.

**Stream 3 — EBA Transparency Exercise.** The European Banking Authority publishes standardised financial data for 156+ EU banks every year. This is gold for benchmarking — every bank on the same basis. 1,463 training pairs from one CSV file.

**Stream 4 — FDIC Call Reports.** Every US bank files quarterly with the FDIC. Public REST API. Top 100 banks by assets. 935 pairs.

**Stream 5 — Rating Agency Methodology.** Fitch, S&P, DBRS Morningstar methodologies. Basel Committee full suite (9 documents), OCC Comptroller's Handbook (7 volumes), FDIC examination manuals, EBA SREP guidelines, PRA supervisory statements, OSFI, APRA prudential standards. These teach the model the analytical language and regulatory thresholds.

**Stream 6 — Real Analyst Credit Papers.** The gold standard. Real S&P, Moody's and Fitch credit reports covering Lloyds, Barclays, Deutsche Bank, Standard Chartered and others. Dropped into `credit_reports/` and extracted automatically. Weighted 2× in training. These set the quality standard everything else trains toward.

Total: **~5,000 training pairs** from 150+ source documents across the US, UK, EU, Australia and Canada.

## How Training Pairs Work

This is the part that confused me most when starting out. Worth explaining properly.

A training pair is simply an example of the behaviour you want the model to learn. It has three parts — system, user, assistant — in a standard instruction-following format. Here is a simplified real example:

```
System:
  You are a senior credit analyst specialising in bank credit analysis using
  the CAMELS framework. Every numerical claim must cite a source as [Source: p.XX].
  If data is unavailable write 'Data not available' — never fabricate figures.
  Structure responses with: Assessment (Strong/Adequate/Weak/Critical), Key Metrics,
  Analysis, Peer Context, Key Risks, and source citations.

User:
  Bank: Lloyds Banking Group
  Reporting Year: 2025

  TASK: Analyse the Capital Adequacy section from the following extract.
  Cite specific page numbers for every figure. Assess as Strong/Adequate/Weak/Critical.

  KEY METRICS EXTRACTED:
    CET1 RATIO: 14.0% [Source: p.53]
      ← 7th decile globally (median: 13.8%, p10: 10.5%, p90: 14.2%; n=847 bank-years)
    LEVERAGE RATIO: 5.4% [Source: p.53]
    TOTAL CAPITAL RATIO: 20.1% [Source: p.53]

  TEXT CONTENT:
  [Page 53]
  The Group's CET1 ratio was 14.0% at 31 December 2025, compared with 13.5%
  at 31 December 2024, reflecting strong organic capital generation...
  [Page 54]
  The leverage ratio was 5.4%, well above the 3.25% minimum requirement...
