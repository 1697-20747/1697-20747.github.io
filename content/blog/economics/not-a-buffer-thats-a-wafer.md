## The debate: "not a buffer, that is a wafer"

On 10 September 2026 the House of Lords held a general debate on the UK's fiscal outlook, moved by Lord Bridges of Headley (Conservative).((Parallel Parliament, [Fiscal Outlook, Lords Chamber, 10 September 2026](https://www.parallelparliament.co.uk/lord/lord-bridges-of-headley/debate/2026-09-10/lords/lords-chamber/fiscal-outlook).)) It's worth a read in full, but the short version is that the numbers are not subtle.

Lord Bridges opened with the stock: national debt is nearly £3 trillion, 94% of GDP, up almost £96 billion in the past year, which he called "on an unsustainable path." His most quotable line was about the fiscal headroom the Chancellor is working with — down to roughly £5 billion, which he characterised as "not a buffer; that is a wafer." Fair, and also a genuinely good line, which is presumably why every summary of the debate leads with it, this one included.

The market side of his argument is the more interesting bit for this blog. He pointed out that the UK now has the highest 10-year borrowing costs in the G7, and the fourth-highest among advanced economies globally, and that a recent 30-year gilt sale cleared at the highest borrowing cost seen in almost three decades. Debt servicing is forecast at £110 billion for 2025-26 — 3.5% of national income, more than the education budget and closing in on double the defence budget — rising to £137 billion by 2030-31.

His diagnosis of *why* runs through the usual Opposition lines: higher business taxes and the Employment Rights Act (which he put at roughly £1 billion a year in direct cost) weakening growth, welfare spending up £75 billion a year with health and disability benefits up over a third, and the OBR's own estimate of potential growth stuck at 1.5% a year, with GDP per capita growing at 1.1% — roughly half the pre-financial-crisis trend. Other peers filled in around him: Lord Rooker and Lord Davies on the demographic backdrop, Lord Elliott on welfare dependency, Lord Howell on the fiscal cost of decarbonisation, and Lord Hill on the more general point that somebody in government needs to be honest in public about the trade-offs, rather than just in the OBR's small print. Bridges closed on the classic bond-market threat: defer the hard choices and the market makes them for you, "more painful and more costly" than if a Chancellor had chosen the timing.

None of this is new territory for this blog — the G20 sovereign-spreads post earlier this year covered the "vigilantes are voting" side of the story((See this blog's earlier piece on [G20 sovereign credit spreads](/blog/economics/g20-sovereign-credit-spreads/).)) — but a debate this gilt-market-heavy is a good excuse to actually look at what today's curve is pricing, and then do something Lord Bridges didn't: pick one real bond and show what borrowing-cost volatility does to an actual buyer, in cash terms, split into what came from interest and what came from capital.

## The curve itself

Here's the UK gilt curve as it stood on 11 September 2026, benchmark yields from 1 month out to 30 years.((Trading Economics, [United Kingdom Government Bond Yield](https://tradingeconomics.com/united-kingdom/government-bond-yield), accessed 13 September 2026.)) The top panel is the par yield curve; the bottom panel converts it into a discount factor curve — the price today of £1 received at each future date, which is the actual "discount curve" the title promised.

{{< figure src="/images/economics/fiscal-outlook-discount-curve.png" caption="UK gilt par yield curve and implied discount factors, 11 September 2026" >}}

The shape matches Bridges' framing precisely: short rates around 3.85–4.9%, and a long end that keeps climbing all the way to 30 years (5.91%) rather than flattening or inverting the way a "normal" curve eventually would. That persistent long-end premium — the market wanting more compensation the further out it lends to the UK government — is exactly the "term premium" story behind his "highest 30-year borrowing cost in almost three decades" line. £1 due in 10 years is worth about 59p today; £1 due in 30 years is worth about 17p. That second number is doing a lot of the work in "debt servicing to £137 billion by 2030-31" — every pound the DMO borrows long-dated from here gets discounted (from the buyer's side) and compounded (from the taxpayer's side) at close to 6%, not the 3–4% that was on offer for most of the 2010s.

One methodological note, in the spirit of flagging simplifications rather than pretending they don't exist: this is a *par* yield curve, not a bootstrapped zero curve. I've fitted it with a monotone (PCHIP) interpolation and then used the fitted par yield at each maturity as a proxy for the zero rate, discounting semi-annually. For a curve this smooth the difference between a proper bootstrapped zero curve and this shortcut is small — a few basis points at most maturities — but it's a shortcut, not a bootstrap, and a trading desk would not accept it as one.

## Finding an actual 3% gilt — and a better story than planned

The original brief here was to find a real UK gilt issued at a 3.00% coupon and reprice it off the curve above. Worth being upfront: **there is no conventional gilt currently in issue with an exact 3.00% coupon** — I checked the DMO's own gilts-in-issue data and several market aggregators, and the coupon ladder runs …2.5%, 3.25%, 3.5%, 3.75%… with nothing sitting on exactly 3.00%.((Cross-checked against [giltsyield.com's conventional gilts in issue](https://giltsyield.com/bond/) and [Hargreaves Lansdown's UK gilt price list](https://www.hl.co.uk/shares/corporate-bonds-gilts/bond-prices/uk-gilts).)) A closer look at the DMO's full **Gilt Issuance History** (report D2.1E)((UK Debt Management Office, [Gilt Issuance History, report D2.1E](https://www.dmo.gov.uk/data/pdfdatareport?reportCode=D2.1E), data as of 13 September 2026.)) turned up something more useful than a coupon-matching exercise: a currently-outstanding gilt issued in tranches across the 2022–24 hiking cycle, cheap enough at several points to sit close to a 3%-ish entry yield, that can actually be marked to market against the curve above rather than treated as a closed, historical case.

That gilt is the **3¾% Treasury Gilt 2038** (ISIN GB00BQC4R999), maturing 29 January 2038 — still about 11.4 years out from today.((UK Debt Management Office, [3¾% Treasury Gilt 2038 issue prospectus](https://dmo.gov.uk/media/buxa3fvp/prosp091122a.pdf) — coupon dates 29 January/29 July, maturity 29 January 2038.)) It first came to market via syndication on 9 November 2022 at £96.33 (yield 4.075%), then was reopened by ordinary "outright" auction eight more times through November 2024, with issue prices swinging from £96.63 down to £88.56 depending on where the market happened to be that week — yields at issue ranging from 4.07% to 4.87%.((All issue prices, yields and nominal amounts from the DMO Gilt Issuance History, report D2.1E.)) An earlier draft of this post used a different, similarly-timed gilt for this exercise, but that one had already matured and redeemed at par before today's valuation date — meaning there was no live market price left to sell it at. This one is still very much alive, which is the point: the brief asked for a bond *sold at today's curve*, not one held to maturity, and that only works with something still trading.

## Nine entry points, one sale price

An **entry point**, here, just means one of the nine separate occasions — one syndication and eight ordinary "outright" auctions, 9 November 2022 through 20 November 2024 — on which an investor could actually buy this gilt when it was issued, each at its own price and yield. Reprice the 3¾% 2038's remaining cash flows off the same curve as the chart above, and it comes out to **£86.27 clean today** (£86.74 dirty), equivalent to a flat yield of 5.37% — modified duration is about 8.9 years at that level. Every one of the nine tranches gets marked to that same exit price, because it's the same bond; the only thing that differs between entry points is what an investor originally paid to get in.

{{< figure src="/images/economics/fiscal-outlook-2038-gilt-tranche-returns.png" caption="If sold today off the current curve: capital return, interest received, and CPI-real return, by 2022–24 issuance tranche of the 3¾% Treasury Gilt 2038 (GB00BQC4R999)" >}}

This is where the arithmetic from earlier in this post actually bites. **Capital return** — (today's £86.27 minus the issue price) divided by the issue price — is negative for every single tranche, because every tranche was issued at a lower yield than the 5.37% the curve implies today: from a relatively mild −2.6% (the October 2023 tranche, issued cheapest at £88.56/4.87%) to a brutal −10.7% (the January 2024 tranche, issued expensive at £96.63/4.07%, right before the market moved hard against it). **Interest received** — coupons actually paid from issue to today, simply summed — is positive throughout, ranging from £7.8 (the shortest-held August 2024 tranche) to £15.6 (the November 2022 tranche, held longest). Net the two together and total nominal return ranges from a still-decent **+5.1%** (November 2022, the earliest and longest-held tranche) down to **−2.4%** (August 2024) — the only tranche in this set with an outright nominal loss, because it was both expensive at issue and short-held.

Adjust for inflation — again with each tranche's own issue month as the CPI reference point, not a single common start date — and the picture gets considerably worse. Deflating by ONS CPI (all items, 2015=100), from each tranche's issue-month value up to 142.5 (June 2026, the latest published reading)((Office for National Statistics, [CPI Index 00: All Items 2015=100](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/inflationandpriceindices/timeseries/d7bt/mm23).)) turns **every tranche but one** negative in real terms: only the October 2023 tranche — the cheapest entry point of the whole run — comes out ahead in real terms, at +2.0%. Every other tranche shows a real loss, from a relatively contained −0.7% down to −8.0% (August 2024 again, nominal loss compounded by inflation on top). Buying a UK government bond at a fixed 3¾% coupon, over this particular stretch of the rate cycle, has cost real money for eight buyers out of nine — not because anything went wrong with the UK's credit standing, but purely because the curve moved against them after they bought.

## What this has to do with Lord Bridges

This is the same mechanism as the debate itself, just run from the lender's side of the ledger. Lord Bridges' complaint is about the *government's* cost of borrowing rising as gilt yields climb — the highest G7 10-year cost, the worst 30-year auction in three decades. The 2038 gilt's tranche history is the mirror image: nine ordinary auctions across two years, at yields 200-plus basis points apart, have left most of the buyers who financed that borrowing sitting on a real-terms loss today if they wanted their money back before 2038. Nobody's credit judgement was wrong at any of the nine auctions — the UK was not a materially different credit risk in November 2022 than in November 2024 — the entire spread in outcomes is the yield curve moving under everyone's feet in real time, which is exactly the instability Bridges was describing from the Treasury's side of the same ledger. Higher gilt yields cut both ways: bad for the government's future borrowing costs, as he said, and just as bad, right now, for the pension funds, insurers and ordinary savers who bought the government's existing debt at the old, lower rate.

## The caveats, because they matter

A few things worth being explicit about before anyone treats these numbers as more precise than they are:

- The par-curve-as-zero-curve shortcut noted earlier in this post applies to today's £86.27 exit price too — a proper bootstrapped zero curve would move it by a handful of basis points, not by anything that changes the story.
- Interest is simply summed, not reinvested or compounded. A true total-return calculation would reinvest each coupon at prevailing short rates as it was received; that would shift the figures a little, but not enough to reorder which tranches did better or worse relative to each other.
- No accrued-interest adjustment is made at purchase; the exit price is on a clean-price basis with accrued interest handled separately (£0.47 as of 13 September 2026, on the last 22 Apr/22 Oct-style coupon cycle — see the discounting detail in the curve section).
- Real returns use ONS CPI (all items, 2015=100) as the deflator, with each tranche's own issue month as the base, per the earlier framing in this post. CPI is only published with a lag — June 2026 (142.5) is the latest available reading as of writing, not September 2026 itself, so the real-return figures above are very slightly understated if inflation has continued since June. RPI or CPIH would also give marginally different numbers; CPI is the Bank of England's own target measure, which is the more defensible choice for a UK rates post.
- Two further tranches of this gilt (16 July 2024 and 15 April 2025) are excluded from the chart: the DMO's issuance history records their nominal amounts but not a separately published clean price or yield, so a capital-return figure for them can't be built from this dataset alone.
- As SVB found out, you can get cleaned out holding a riskless asset if you don't understand duration.
