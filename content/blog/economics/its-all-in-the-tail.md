## Overview

This post and analysis is something of a waste of time, as we all already know the answer. Still its worth laying out the data.

The hypothesis is the foreign exchange rates are not normally distributed, and that the shorter the interval, the less normal they are. It is widely recognized that the normal distribution does not adequately capture the fat-tailed and often skewed nature of financial returns. Early empirical studies documenting these non-normal characteristics—such as heavy tails and excess kurtosis relative to the normal distribution—include work by Mandelbrot (1963), Fama (1965), Farber et al. (1977), Westerfield (1977), and McFarland et al. (1982), among others.

Mandelbrot will be a post in and of itself, along with some ubiquitous matching pretty pictures.

## Whats normal anyways?

The normal distribution (or Gaussian distribution) is a symmetrical, bell-shaped probability distribution where most data points cluster around a central mean, with frequency decreasing as values move further away. It is defined by its mean (𝜇) and standard deviation (𝜎), representing real-world phenomena like heights, test scores, and errors.

## Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7)

| Range from Mean | Approx. % of Data | Description |
|-----------------|-------------------|-------------|
| ±1σ             | ≈ 68%             | Within 1 standard deviation of the mean |
| ±2σ             | ≈ 95%             | Within 2 standard deviations of the mean |
| ±3σ             | ≈ 99.7%           | Within 3 standard deviations of the mean |

## Why would FX rates be normal?

Real world is the key phrase here. It implies constraints. There are lots of people in the world. Of all of those people, sticking with homo sapiens, some are rather tall. There will be few cases of unfortunate persons with pituitary gland problems (such as pituitary gigantism) who might be exceptionally tall. Very tall indeed. But no one is 1,000 ft tall, or 100 ft tall. Biology applies constraints. No tree is 10,000 ft tall either, it would collapse under its own weight. Brownian motion, is similarly constrained. No speck of dust if going to jump from out under the microscope slide and out the window. as a reminder Brownian motion is the random, erratic movement of microscopic particles suspended in a fluid (liquid or gas). This is caused by constant collisions with fast-moving atoms or molecules. Observed in 1827 by Robert Brown (hence the name) and later explained by Albert Einstein, this phenomenon provides direct evidence for the existence of atoms and particle theory. It is named after the botanist Robert Brown, who was interested in modelling the motion of pollen in water. The pollen particles are randomly kicked around by water molecules and perform Brownian motion. I literally consider Brownian motion of pollen particles in the air at the commencement of every hay fever season. So financial markets are dominated by random walks prices from a botanist!

Now, the minds of men are not constrained by gravity, and are capable of maintaining delusion. Hence prices may experience jumps beyond what fundamentals of ordered processes might suggest. Or valuations. once the delusion is removed price can collapse, or the reverse. I am not suggesting the FX rates do not reflect underlying economic realities, quite the opposite. But the constraints on prices of anything, can be less that in coherent biological or physical systems. Hence we should neither expect, or be shocked, by extreme shifts in prices in the near term.
