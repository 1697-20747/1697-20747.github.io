## Overview

Leopold Aschenbrenner was 22 when he wrote "Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead" in June 2024, a widely read essay arguing that AGI was closer than markets priced and that whoever controlled the compute build-out would control the century. He'd just left OpenAI's superalignment team, reportedly after a dispute over leaked information. 
He turned the essay into a fund, seeded with $225 million, named it after the essay, and set about betting his own thesis with other people's money. 
Apparently, I don't know, I have never read it. Good luck to him.

It worked. Spectacularly, for a while. Situational Awareness LP scaled to roughly $45 billion in assets under management by July 2026, posting net returns of 439% in H1 2026 alone and over 1,000% since inception. 
Then, in the space of about three weeks in July 2026, it gave back roughly $35 billion of that $45 billion, was forced to sell its entire public equity book to Ken Griffin's Citadel at distressed prices, and shrank to something closer to $10 billion — most of that in a single illiquid private stake that happens to be exempt from margin calls. 
Focus very hard on the last part of that sentence, such facts matter, giving us potential insight to the structural flaws of the fund's documentation. 
$10 billion is still more billions than I have, so it's not for me to criticize him as a person or his achievements.

I'm not here to relitigate the AGI thesis. Aschenbrenner may yet be right about the decade. I'm here because this is a clean, almost textbook case study in what leverage does to a correct thesis, and why "correct, eventually" and "solvent, currently" are two entirely different questions that credit people are paid to keep separate. 
This is not the first time a genius has screwed something up, and it won't be the last time. 
Or a non-genius. 
To generalize this is typically the case where the non-genius type who are determined to screw things up go into government, so they can do it with other people's money.
Perhaps they are not so stupid after all....

Experience matters. Experience at what becomes the key point. 
A concentrated fund is none of my business. 
A single name or single sector concentrated fund with a lot of leverage is firmly in scope of the lessons learned laid out herein.

This is self-inflicted fragility, plain and simple.
It screams hubris.

### The thesis, and the trade

Aschenbrenner's public book was a concentrated, leveraged bet on AI infrastructure demand: long semiconductor and datacenter names including SK Hynix, CoreWeave, Nebius, Micron and Sandisk, with bearish positioning on AI-adjacent software names like Adobe on the view that the value accrues to the picks-and-shovels layer, not the application layer. 
This is not a stupid thesis. 
Plenty of serious allocators hold some version of it.
Raising a fund is also not nothing.
Perhaps it reflects a macro environment where too much money is chasing too few versions of the same idea.
But have you tried buying DRAM of late, those prices are insane.
By which I mean what goes stratospherically up tends not to be stable.
Something is occurring in the real world that is not to be dismissed. 
What made it fragile wasn't the direction of the bet, it was the size of it relative to the fund's actual staying power.
That and a potentially weak set of documents from the fund's perspective.
Good job credit officers and lawyers for the other side of the trade though.

### The mechanics of the unwind

The fund ran approximately 4x leverage on its public equity positions. At 4x, a 25% decline in the underlying book is sufficient to wipe out investor equity entirely, mechanically, before anyone gets to debate whether the long-run thesis is still intact. In July 2026, the concentrated AI infrastructure names fell somewhere between 35% and 47% in a single month. That is not a black swan. That is a normal, unremarkable drawdown for a concentrated tech basket in a risk-off month. The leverage is what turned an ordinary correction into extinction.

> Lending is more about the return of capital than the return on capital. The same is true of leverage generally: an extra turn of return is slim comfort when the position is gone before the thesis has time to be right. Fragility is almost always a self-inflicted disease.

Prime brokers Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Bank of America issued margin calls as collateral values fell. 
Because they are smart, not the borrower's friends, **and because their trading documentation permitted this course of action**.
Once multiple prime brokers are calling collateral on the same concentrated names at the same time, you don't get an orderly unwind, you get a fire sale, because everyone is trying to sell the same illiquid-at-scale positions into the same falling market simultaneously.

The movie screening is paused, fire has been announced, lenders are all in the same mad rush for the theatre exits. As they should be.

On 30 July 2026, Situational Awareness sold its entire remaining public portfolio to Citadel, "below market" by most accounts, which is the going rate for a forced seller with no other bidders on a Thursday.

## Why Citadel, and why this keeps happening

Citadel buying the distressed book at below-market prices is not an act of charity, and it's not a new pattern for Griffin's firm. Distressed, forced-seller inventory is exactly the liquidity Citadel exists to price and absorb — the seller needs to transact today regardless of price, the buyer has the balance sheet and the patience to wait for fair value to reassert itself, and the spread between those two facts is the trade. It's the same basic logic that shows up in every forced-unwind story: the desperate party doesn't get market price, they get the price of desperation, and someone with dry powder is always on the other side waiting to collect that spread. Aschenbrenner's fund becomes, for now, the year's most well-documented illustration of that spread being collected.

It's also worth separating two things that get conflated whenever a fund like this blows up: being wrong, and being unable to survive being temporarily wrong. Situational Awareness may not have been wrong about AI infrastructure demand over a ten-year horizon. It was unable to survive a five-week drawdown at 4x leverage on that view. Those are different failures, and only one of them is a failure of the thesis.

## Experience Matters

Experience at what?
Is it reasonable to expect someone with little to no experience in financial markets to understand the minutia of traded products and prime broking documents?
No.
It's the same reason if you are smart, you don't try and repair your gas heater. You should know you lack the knowledge and insights that **can only be acquired through experience**, and as a consequence you don't even try.
Because the fragility in that space is blowing your house to bits.

It's likely, one hopes, that legal counsel for the fund was appropriate.
If not, that is the first major error.
That said, it's counsel's job to do what you ask them to.
If you don't know enough to prime them to take extreme care around margining rights, more fool you, not more fool your counsel.
We were not in the room, so none of us can know.

Especially because of the focus and potential thought trap of the thesis, running the leverage was not a simple choice.
Well, it was, but the consequences were not.
I wonder if a basic stress price move analysis was ever really discussed.

> As the advice goes, if you don't really know what you are doing, STOP!

Then get someone who does.
The real trick might be controlling your ego so you can recognize that you may require expertise.

## Key Takeaways

- Leverage doesn't care how right you are. At 4x, a 25% drawdown is a wipeout regardless of whether the thesis is validated eighteen months later.
- Concentration plus leverage plus a single risk-off month is a standard recipe. This is not exotic. It has happened before, under different tickers, and will happen again.
- A thesis being correct on a ten-year view and a fund being solvent on a ten-week view are separate questions. Credit and risk functions exist specifically to keep those two questions from getting merged into one.
- The forced seller never gets the market price. Ever. Learn this until it's etched into your mind. Book value is a fantasy in an illiquid market. Someone with patient capital is always priced to take the other side of that.
- Conviction is not a substitute for a survivable capital structure. Twenty-five years old or fifty-five, the maths of margin calls doesn't check your resume before it clears the account.
Your ability to detach and see the big picture is probably non-existent arising from your focused thesis. The very conviction that made you (initially successful) blinds you to the bigger picture through long established psychological processes.

Borrowed money is not yours. Lenders tend to want it back. 
You probably cannot control, manage or avoid macro shocks. 
Leverage is a choice that you do control.
