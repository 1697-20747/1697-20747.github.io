## The Floor Worth More Than the Building

NYU Stern professor and entrepreneur Scott Galloway ran the numbers during what he came to call his "WeWTF" research series — a set of analyses published between 2017 and 2019 — and found something that should have stopped the whole circus cold.

In several WeWork buildings, the single floor WeWork leased was, based on the company's implied enterprise valuation, worth more than the entire building that contained it.

Not a rounding error. Not a nuance. The arithmetic was straightforward: take the $47 billion enterprise value, divide by leased square footage, apply the resulting per-square-foot implied value to the floors WeWork occupied — and you get a per-floor figure that exceeded what you would pay to buy the whole building outright. The building next door, identical in every material respect, was simply valued at what it was: real estate.

Galloway called it "WeWTF." The name was appropriate.

This is now well-documented history. But the lessons from WeWork are not the standard corporate scandal lessons. The misconduct, to the extent it is accurately characterised as such, is almost beside the point. The more instructive question is how a real estate business with negative margins convinced the world it deserved a technology company valuation, and why the system produced no meaningful resistance until the IPO roadshow collapsed in front of a live audience.

> Scott Galloway writes at [profgalloway.com](https://www.profgalloway.com/wewtf/). The original WeWTF posts remain available. Worth reading in full — the analysis is precise, and the register is characteristically direct.

*Refer also to my earlier post on the language of cults. WeWork's internal communication culture maps with uncomfortable precision to the framework laid out there.*

## The Setup

WeWork was founded in 2010 by Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey. The business model was, in essence, straightforward: lease office space on long-term contracts from landlords, subdivide and sublease it to individuals and small businesses on short-term flexible terms, and pocket the spread.

That is not a technology business. That is a real estate arbitrage operation with duration mismatch embedded in its structure. The liabilities are long-term and fixed. The revenue is short-term and variable. This is a structurally dangerous position. If occupancy drops, cash burn accelerates immediately. If a pandemic arrives, tenants disappear overnight. The business is a leveraged bet on the perpetual, uninterrupted demand for short-term flexible office space — a bet with asymmetric downside and no structural hedge.

SoftBank's Vision Fund began investing in 2017 and continued through subsequent rounds. By January 2019, WeWork carried a $47 billion implied valuation driven by SoftBank's own investment marks. At its peak, WeWork was the largest leaseholder of commercial office space in both New York City and London.

The S-1 filing in August 2019 provided the first proper public view of the numbers. What it showed was $1.9 billion in net losses on $1.8 billion in revenue in 2018. Gross margins were under 20 percent. The business had never generated a profit. The IPO was withdrawn within weeks. Neumann departed the following month with a settlement reported at approximately $1.7 billion.

## The Tech Company Fiction

The mechanism that produced a $47 billion valuation for a business losing money at scale was conceptually simple: call it a technology company.

Technology companies trade at revenue multiples of ten, twenty times or more, because the market prices in scalability. The marginal cost of adding a new software customer approaches zero. The product ships without a lease negotiation, a fit-out, or a building manager. The margin profile is fundamentally different.

WeWork was not this. WeWork signed fifteen-year leases on Manhattan real estate to sublease to tenants who could leave on a month's notice. The comparable companies it invited analysts to consider — Stripe, Slack, Dropbox — ran gross margins of sixty to eighty percent plus. Not one of them paid rent on millions of square feet across three hundred cities.

To help bridge this gap, WeWork's management invented a metric. They called it **Community Adjusted EBITDA**.

Community Adjusted EBITDA excluded rent, tenancy costs, building staff salaries, amenity costs, sales and marketing expenses, and general and administrative expenses. In other words, it excluded most of the costs of running the actual business. The 2018 figure, as measured by this construct, was more than double what standard EBITDA would have produced once straight-line rent was added back. The S-1 drew immediate and widespread ridicule. The metric quietly disappeared from the final draft.

The logic and limitations of EBITDA as a cash flow proxy are well explored in the Moody's paper referenced in the EBITDA post on this site. The WeWork variant was a creative extension of an already frequently abused concept. Standard EBITDA, at least, excludes only financing costs and non-cash charges — both defensible in specific analytical contexts. Community Adjusted EBITDA excluded the central operating cost of the entire enterprise.

> Any time a borrower or issuer introduces a custom profitability metric that diverges materially from standard accounting measures, the purpose is almost always to redirect attention from the line items that matter most. The appropriate response is not to engage with the metric on its own terms. The appropriate response is to go back to the income statement.

WeWork wanted to be valued like software. It operated like a leveraged REIT with long liabilities, short revenues, and zero competitive moat. Any adequately capitalised competitor could replicate the model. None of the structural advantages that justify premium technology valuations — network effects, switching costs, marginal cost approaching zero — were present.

## The Landlord and the Tenant

Concurrent with the valuation mechanics was a more straightforward governance problem.

While serving as CEO, Adam Neumann personally acquired buildings and leased them back to WeWork. The company, which he controlled through super-voting share structures, paid him rent. His board — which he also effectively controlled — approved the arrangements as related-party transactions.

A specific documented example: Neumann and fashion designer Elie Tahari acquired the building at 88 University Place, New York, in 2015 for approximately $70 million. They renovated it and leased the space to WeWork. WeWork paid Neumann rent on property he owned.

The scale was not marginal. WeWork paid more than $12 million in rent on officer-owned properties in 2016 and 2017 alone. Contracted future payments to officer-owned properties totalled approximately $110 million over the terms of the leases, per the company's own debt offering prospectus.

Separately, following the rebrand to The We Company, it emerged that Neumann personally held the trademark to the word "We." The company paid him approximately $5.9 million to licence it back. The payment was reversed following public disclosure, but the fact that it occurred at all — and required external pressure rather than board intervention to reverse — is informative about the governance environment the board was operating in.

The board's characterisation of these arrangements as "related-party transactions, reviewed and approved" is technically accurate. It is not a defence. The question of who watches the watchers is precisely the question that governance structures exist to answer. In WeWork's case, the answer was that nobody with meaningful independence was watching.

> Related-party transactions are not inherently problematic. They are, however, a reliable early warning signal. When management's personal financial interests are directly intertwined with corporate decisions, the analytical starting assumption should be adversarial rather than deferential. Board approval is evidence of process. It is not evidence of propriety.

## The Allegations

A note on precision. No criminal charges were filed against Adam Neumann in connection with the IPO process or the valuation methodology described above. Media characterisations of WeWork as straightforward fraud conflate several distinct things, and it is worth being careful here.

There is a separate and documented legal matter: a Florida real estate executive was convicted in 2024 of a $77 million fraud scheme connected to a fabricated bid for WeWork assets in bankruptcy proceedings. This is unrelated to Neumann personally.

What is documented, and not seriously contested, is the following. Neumann extracted more than $700 million in personal liquidity from WeWork before the IPO attempt, through share sales and other mechanisms, according to Wall Street Journal reporting. SoftBank paid him approximately $1.7 billion to exit the board and sever his formal ties to the company following the IPO collapse — a settlement that rewarded the person most responsible for the outcome at the direct expense of investors who remained.

CNBC reported at the time that the $47 billion valuation was "always a fiction created by SoftBank." This is the more analytically interesting point. SoftBank's Vision Fund had $100 billion to deploy. When that volume of capital exists with an aggressive mandate and compressed time horizon, price discovery breaks. WeWork's peak valuation was not a market clearing price produced by competing buyers and sellers. It was a mark set by a single investor whose incentive structure rewarded optimistic projections. The credit and debt markets — which funded WeWork's $669 million unsecured bond issuance in 2018 — treated that mark as information. It was not.

WeWork filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on 6 November 2023. The market capitalisation at that point was approximately $45 million. The stock was delisted in January 2024.

From $47 billion to $45 million is not a rounding error.

## The Cost to SoftBank

SoftBank's Vision Fund began investing in WeWork in August 2017. Masayoshi Son met Neumann, spoke to him for twelve minutes, and wrote a $4.4 billion cheque. On his way to the car, Son reportedly told Neumann he needed to "think bigger." Subsequent rounds brought SoftBank's total equity investment to $10 billion by January 2019 — the same month the $47 billion valuation was set.

When the IPO collapsed, SoftBank assembled an October 2019 rescue package: $5 billion in new debt financing, an accelerated $1.5 billion equity commitment that had been scheduled for 2020, and a $3 billion tender offer for existing shareholders. This pushed the equity total to approximately $13 billion and total exposure including debt to roughly $16 billion. SoftBank also paid Neumann approximately $1.7 billion to exit the board — the $185 million consulting fee, cash to cover his $500 million JPMorgan credit line, and associated payments.

The final accounting came in SoftBank's July–September 2023 earnings, reported just after the bankruptcy filing. SoftBank disclosed a cumulative loss on WeWork exceeding $14 billion — approximately $11.5 billion in equity losses and a further $2.2 billion written off on debt. The remaining stake was taken to zero in Q4 2023. Bloomberg estimated that Neumann personally extracted more than $2.1 billion from WeWork across his tenure through share sales, the exit settlement, and the consulting arrangement.

Masayoshi Son described the investment as "a stain on my life."

There is one coda worth noting. SoftBank subsequently realised more than $16 billion from its stake in Arm Holdings following Arm's 2023 IPO — a gain that arithmetically exceeded the total WeWork loss. Son's portfolio survived the bet. That does not rehabilitate the decision. A firm that can absorb a $14 billion write-down because another position happened to work is a firm with a risk management problem it got away with. Getting away with it is not the same as not having it.

## Credit Risk Lessons

The WeWork story contains several lessons that do not require embellishment.

**Tech multiples do not belong on real estate cash flows.** If the revenue disappears the moment tenants walk — and short-lease tenants can always walk — the underlying business is a real estate operation. Dress it how you like; value it accordingly. The duration mismatch between long fixed liabilities and short variable revenues is not a feature of the model. It is the central risk factor, and it played out exactly as duration mismatches tend to when conditions deteriorate.

**Covenant-lite lending is not a strategy.** WeWork raised $669 million in unsecured bonds in 2018. Investors accepted thin covenants because yield was scarce and the SoftBank mark provided apparent comfort. Those bonds traded at cents on the dollar. High yield without appropriate structural protection is high risk with the optionality removed.

**Related-party transactions require adversarial analysis.** The board's approval of Neumann's various landlord arrangements did not make them arms-length. When management's personal financial interests are directly intertwined with corporate decisions, the analyst's job is to model the worst case and assume it is more likely than the best case.

**Invented metrics deserve zero analytical credit.** Community Adjusted EBITDA is the canonical recent example of a custom profitability metric designed to obscure rather than illuminate. The pattern recurs. Any metric that excludes the core operating costs of the business it purports to measure should be discarded, not adjusted.

**Soft money distorts hard prices.** The Vision Fund's $47 billion mark was produced by an entity deploying $100 billion with a mandate to move decisively. That is not a market clearing price. It is a strategic bet made by a party whose incentives differed materially from ordinary debt and equity investors. Independent analysis that deferred to that mark without questioning its basis was not analysis.

**Duration mismatch is always dangerous.** The WeWork business model was structurally identical in its risk profile to funding long-term assets with short-term liabilities. The liabilities were lease obligations rather than deposits. The mechanism of failure when conditions deteriorate is the same in either case.

## The Building, Revisited

The building that housed the WeWork floor mathematically valued above it did not go bankrupt.

It did not require a $1.7 billion settlement for its CEO. It did not introduce a bespoke accounting metric to explain why losing money constituted a superior form of winning. It was not governed by a structure in which the CEO simultaneously served as landlord, trademark licensor, and majority voting shareholder.

Galloway described the WeWork valuation as "arguably the most expensive mistake in the history of capital markets." That may or may not be the precise characterisation. It is not hyperbole.

WeWork is not primarily a story about co-working spaces. It is a story about what happens when a single floor is valued above the building that contains it, and the people in the room with the authority to say so chose not to.

---

## Key Takeaways

- A business with long fixed liabilities and short variable revenues is a duration mismatch. Model the failure mode before investing in the equity or the debt.
- Custom profitability metrics that exclude core operating costs are a signal. The signal is not positive.
- SoftBank's $47 billion mark was not a market price. Analysts who deferred to it without independent verification were not doing their jobs.
- Related-party transactions require adversarial rather than deferential scrutiny. Board approval is not sufficient.
- Galloway's arithmetic was available to anyone who wanted to look. Most people in the room had strong financial reasons not to.
