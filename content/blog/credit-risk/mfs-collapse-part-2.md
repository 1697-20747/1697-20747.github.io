*This is Part 2 of a series on the collapse of Market Financial Solutions (MFS). [Part 1: MFS Collapse](/blog/credit-risk/mfs-collapse/) covers the background, the double-pledging allegations, the creditor list, the accountant, and the position as at 28 April 2026. This post picks up from there and focuses on recoveries.*

> **A note on allegations.** The allegations in this post come from court filings by the MFS administrators and from reporting of those filings. They have not been tested at trial. Mr Raja denies them and says there was no fraud or dishonesty. A freezing order is an interim measure to preserve assets while a claim is decided. It is not a finding of wrongdoing. Nothing here should be read as a statement that any individual has committed any offence.

---

## 1. Overview

In Part 1 the question was *how*. Seven months after administration, the more pressing question for the lenders is *how much comes back*. The short answer is: very little.

Headline position as at late September 2026:

| Item | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Total owed to creditors | ~£2.3 billion (up from an earlier ~£1.8bn estimate) | Administrators, via Telegraph, 26 Sep 2026 |
| Cash recovered by administrators to date | ~£700,000 | Administrators, via Telegraph, 26 Sep 2026 |
| Administrator fees approved (not yet drawn) | £8.9 million | Administrators, via Telegraph, 26 Sep 2026 |
| Further administrator fees awaiting approval | ~£5 million | Administrators, via Telegraph, 26 Sep 2026 |
| Amount the administrators allege was misappropriated (denied) | at least £1.3 billion | Administrators' High Court claim, as reported by Bloomberg, 12 May 2026 |
| Amount the administrators allege was paid to personal accounts of Mr Raja and his wife (denied) | £408 million+ | Administrators' High Court claim, as reported by Bloomberg, 12 May 2026 |

Yes, you read that correctly. Recoveries so far come to about **0.03%** of the amount owed. The approved fees alone are more than **12 times** what has been recovered. That is not a criticism of AlixPartners. Tracing assets across four jurisdictions costs money, and the loan book was never going to cover it. It does show how little collateral the administrators have been able to identify and realise so far.

As the saying goes, not all news ages well. This is getting stinkier as time goes on. It is reasonable to expect a lot of regulator oversight on this. For once, it is warranted.

We are getting more information as to what went wrong, and more is coming to light. With a bit of luck, litigation will put credit memos and underwriters' material into the public domain. Sadly, probably not. The key point here is you can imagine someone sane, with a lot of experience, raising an eyebrow at the original request. One can also imagine an aggressive front office type riding roughshod over such observations. The truth is we don't know, yet. Hopefully we do. With every evolution of the facts, the core premise of the structure looks less and less tolerable. From a credit perspective, the structure looks fragile from day one. Whatever the courts eventually decide about the alleged fraud, this does not look like a loss that sound diligence could never have seen coming.

An aside for the uninitiated, recovery is an expensive process. Receivers are skilled labour, they don't and should not work for free. Every time you are moaning about how LGD assumptions mis price your shiny new wonderful loan, please take the time to ponder this case. And don't give the credit officer a hard time. LGD is the mechanism that forces your wonderful loan to contribute to average portfolio losses over time. It's aggregate transfer pricing for long term recovery data. Get over it. That said, this case definitely shows flawed LGD assumptions given the structure, however it is also more UCL than ECL, with the Unexpected Credit Losses dominating the expected. Sound underwriting is supposed to prevent that.

Someone promised this would be high fee and interest earning, all backed by prime secured real estate. Nothing can go wrong, shut up and approve. The underwriting was inadequate. There are two potential cases of misrepresentation here. The underwriting package, and then the real world asset backing data.

It's still early days, let's see what other flawed assumptions and practices come out in the wash.

## 2. What's Actually Left in the Box

The administrators' September filings, as reported, split what remains in MFS's own name as follows:

| Asset | Book value | Administrators' view |
|---|---|---|
| Loans in MFS's name (total) | ~£190 million | "Far lower" in reality |
| – of which bridging loans | £28 million | Recovery uncertain |
| – of which buy-to-let loans | £11 million | More likely to be collectable |
| Proprietary software (cost ~£7m) | — | Worthless; no bidders |
| Funds held by Barclays | ~£13 million | Disputed |
| IT equipment, vehicles, furniture | — | Sold; part of the ~£700k |

Only the bridging and buy-to-let amounts have been broken out in public reporting so far.

For context, Part 1 cited administrators' early estimate of **~£230 million of verifiable collateral** against **~£1.16–1.2 billion** of institutional debt. Six months later, the realisable part of that £230m looks to be shrinking, not growing. It's getting worse, not getting better.

The £7m software write-off deserves a line of its own. Capitalised software at a lender is intangible and firm-specific. It is worth nothing once the firm is gone. Any lender who counted it towards tangible net worth in a covenant test learned that the expensive way.

## 3. Recovery by Lender — What's Been Disclosed

### The funding SPVs: Amber and Zircon

Amber Bridging Ltd and Zircon Bridging Ltd were the MFS-linked funding vehicles that petitioned for the administration. They are now in administration themselves. They sit in the capital structure between the big lenders and the underlying loans. Recovery estimates reported in September 2026 (Bloomberg, via briefs.co, 23 Sep 2026):

| Lender | Vehicle | Advanced | Estimated recovery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atlas SP Partners (Apollo) | Amber & Zircon (combined) | ~£970 million owed to Atlas | £107m – £200m (**~11–21%**) |
| Avenue Capital Group | Amber Bridging | ~£100 million | Administrators "do not expect lenders to recover anything" |
| TPG | Zircon Bridging | £44 million | Nil expected |

Note the gap between Atlas's ~£400m reported exposure and the ~£970m owed to it. This is likely gross funding versus net exposure after back-leverage. Barclays and HSBC sat behind Atlas, as described in Part 1.

Atlas's statement: *"These are public filings from the administrators and our valuation of this position has already accounted for the potential losses referenced in these filings."*

Put simply, the senior secured lender expects to lose roughly 80–90 cents in the pound. The mezzanine and junior money expects to lose all of it.

### The banks

| Lender | Disclosed / reported position | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Barclays | £228m single-name impairment (Q1 2026) | H1 2026 results (28 Jul) still cite the £228m charge; no further MFS-specific charge disclosed. Reported exposure ~£500–600m. |
| HSBC | $400m stage 3 charge (Q1 2026) | "Fraud-related, secondary, securitisation exposure", held indirectly via Apollo back-leverage (see Part 1) |
| Jefferies | ~£103m exposure, including ~$20m loss already taken | Per Bloomberg / press |
| Wells Fargo | ~£143m reported exposure | No specific loss disclosed that I could find |
| Santander | ~$267m reported exposure | No specific loss disclosed that I could find |
| Elliott (via Chetwood Bank) | ~£200m at risk | Exposure through majority ownership of Chetwood, whose book links into MFS structures |
| Private / unsecured investors | ~£169m (a group of private investors) | Coordinating joint action; Begbies Traynor and Coots & Boots appointed to connected entities |

A small reconciliation point. Barclays said in March that its impairment was likely to be "below £500 million". It booked £228m in Q1 and, as far as I can see, nothing more in Q2. On ~£500m of exposure that means Barclays is currently provisioning at roughly a **45% loss**. Atlas, one step down the same chain, is marking recovery at 11–21%. Either Barclays has structural protection (seniority, cash already held, back-leverage terms) that Atlas's position lacks, or there is more provisioning to come.

## 4. The Administrators' Claim: Where Recovery Now Depends

On current numbers, the loan book will not repay anyone meaningfully. Reporting indicates the administrators see their claim against the founder, Paresh Raja, as the main route to any material recovery.

According to Bloomberg's report (12 May 2026), the administrators' High Court claim alleges:

- Misappropriation of **at least £1.3 billion** of funds managed by MFS.
- Payments of **more than £408 million** into personal bank accounts of Mr Raja and his wife in the **UK, Monaco, Singapore and the UAE**.
- Indirect holdings of UK residential property.
- Purchases of a number of luxury cars with MFS funds.

**Mr Raja denies all of these allegations.** His spokesman: *"Mr Raja strongly denies the allegations. He has consistently maintained that there was no fraud or dishonesty."* Earlier, through Mishcon de Reya, he said that *"mistakes have been made but there has been no intention to defraud whatsoever"* (see Part 1). None of the claims has been tested at trial.

A worldwide freezing order is in place. The Telegraph (15 Sep 2026) reports that it limits Mr Raja to **£5,000 a week** in spending without creditor consent. A freezing order preserves assets until the claim is decided. It is not a finding against the person it covers. He is reported to be in Dubai.

### Why this matters for recovery

If the claim succeeds and the assets it describes can be found and realised, the recovery picture changes a lot. Those are big ifs:

1. **Title and priority.** The property is said to be held *indirectly*, through companies. Where more than one lender claims security over the same asset, working out who has priority can take years and cost a great deal.
2. **Jurisdiction.** Any recovery of funds from Monaco, Singapore or the UAE would need recognition and enforcement in each place. A freezing order is one thing. Enforcing a final judgment abroad is a harder test.
3. **Time and cost.** Fees already exceed recoveries more than tenfold. Multi-jurisdiction litigation over several years will add a lot more before any distribution.
4. **Gross vs net.** Any property portfolio will carry its own mortgages. The equity available to MFS creditors could be a small fraction of the gross figure.

## 5. Regulatory Follow-Through

- **FCA enforcement investigation** (opened 20 March 2026) remains open. Its scope is limited by MFS's Annex 1 (AML-only) registration.
- **August 2026 (FT, Martin Arnold):** the FCA announced it will gather data from about **900 Annex 1 firms** it had not previously contacted. Out of ~1,200 Annex 1 firms in total, about 300 were surveyed the year before. Enforcement director Steve Smart said "some Annex 1 firms could be used to facilitate financial crime". Reported follow-ups include longer timelines for new Annex 1 registrations and more scrutiny of SPV lending structures and collateral valuation practices.
- No public Serious Fraud Office or police investigation had been announced as of the time of writing as best as I know. But such things take facts and time to evolve.

## 7. The Credit Lesson: Secured Isn't a Recovery Rate

Part 1 made the point that lending is about the return *of* capital, not the return *on* it. Part 2 puts numbers on it.

A bridging loan secured on London residential property would typically have carried a modelled LGD in the **10–25%** range at origination. The realised position now looks like:

- **Senior secured (Atlas):** ~80–90% loss
- **Junior / mezzanine (Avenue, TPG):** ~100% loss
- **Unsecured / private investors:** ~100% loss, subject to the outcome of the administrators' claim

Collateral protects you only if it exists, is yours alone, and can be enforced. Wherever an asset is pledged more than once, whether by error or otherwise, the second condition fails. Wherever assets sit outside the jurisdiction, the third gets harder. What remains is whatever the lawyers can recover, less what the lawyers cost. Lenders can check all three conditions *before* they advance money. That is a lesson about underwriting, whatever the courts eventually decide about anyone's conduct.

---

## Key Takeaways

- About £700k recovered against £2.3bn owed, seven months in. Fees approved so far are more than 12 times that.
- The senior secured funder expects 11–21% back. The junior lenders expect nothing. Read that again please. This will not fall inside LGD assumptions or modelled data.
- Any material recovery now depends on litigation against the founder across four jurisdictions, and those allegations are denied and untested. The odds of success are unknown, but you can bet it won't come cheap.
- The FCA is widening its look at Annex 1 firms. It is closing the stable door, but at least it is closing it. Fair play to them.
- This is the space private credit wants to play in. Let them, but don't tolerate socializing the losses when this happens again. And again.......
