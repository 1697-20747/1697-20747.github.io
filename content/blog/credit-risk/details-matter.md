## Overview

Underwriting is time consuming, difficult, and sometimes mindless. It is also essential to prevent the destruction of capital.

Sellers are at best honest participants who don't disclose all relevant facts, and at worst liars. Buyers are sometimes fools. Often some combination of all three. This is a story about too much money, not enough sense, and the high cost of being blinded by an icon.

---

## The Building

The Chrysler Building is an icon of industry and Art Deco design — but this is not about the building as architecture. It is about the building as a mechanism for losing money.

It is undeniably beautiful. Every element was bespoke, designed and built by artisans; nothing was ordered from a catalogue. One look at the lift doors should make you ashamed of the skills we have lost. It was less a building, more a *statement* of intent — the tallest in the world for eleven months, until the Empire State Building arrived. The gargoyles alone are individual works of art.

It is also what it is: an Art Deco structure built in 1930. If you have owned a house of that age, you understand that it is also a renovation nightmare. Small windows. Dark interiors. Slow lifts. Column-heavy floor plates. Limited views. A clean glass-faced tower it is not.

For the purchaser discussed here, buying it was one of the dumbest things any institutional investor has ever done. It is a benchmark for poor judgement you do not want to be compared to.

---

## The Land vs. Building Split

A critical and routinely overlooked factor is that **the land and the building have always been owned separately**.

The land was donated to **Cooper Union** (a private New York college) in **1902** — nearly three decades before the building was constructed — and has never been for sale. Every party that has "owned" the Chrysler Building has in fact only held a **ground lease**: they own the structure, collect tenant rents, and pay Cooper Union an annual fee for the land.

This creates a fundamental structural risk. If ground rent rises faster than rental income from tenants, the building becomes a loss-making asset regardless of prestige. That is precisely what happened. Cooper Union raised annual ground rent from **$7.75 million (pre-2018)** to **$32.5 million (2018–2027)**, with a further rise to **$41 million** planned from 2028. RFR ultimately defaulted on over **$21 million** in unpaid rent; Cooper Union terminated the lease in September 2024 and took back control of the building.

### Cooper Union

From their 2023 financial accounts:

> *"The College was founded in 1859 through the bequest of Peter Cooper, a noted industrialist and philanthropist. It offers degree programs in architecture, art, and engineering. The College provided full-tuition scholarships to all students through the year ended June 30, 2014. In April 2013, the Board of Trustees voted to reduce the baseline scholarship to a minimum of 50% for undergraduate students beginning with the class entering in fall 2014."*

It is not suggested that Cooper Union operated outside its remit or the law. But running your building leaseholder into the ground — pun intended — is not a neutral act. No one walks away morally intact from this debacle. Economies require everyone to make some economic profit.

## Ownership & Valuation History

### 1930 — Opening appraisal: $14 million

Upon completion the building was appraised at **$14 million** — less than its $20 million construction cost. It was exempt from city taxes under an 1859 law by virtue of the land being owned by Cooper Union.

---

### 1953 — Sold to William Zeckendorf: $18 million (building only)

Following Walter Chrysler's death in 1940, his family sold the building in 1953. The **$18 million** figure applies to the Chrysler Building alone. The full transaction — which included the Chrysler Annex and the nearby Graybar Building — totalled **$52 million**, then the largest real estate sale in New York City history. Zeckendorf's company Webb & Knapp held a 75% interest; the Graysler Corporation held the remaining 25%.

---

### 1957 — Sold to Lawrence Wien's syndicate: $66 million

The same package of properties sold again in 1957 to **Lawrence Wien's realty syndicate**, setting another New York City record. As in 1953, the price covered multiple buildings, not the Chrysler Building alone.

---

### 1997 — Tishman Speyer acquires: ~$220 million

Tishman Speyer Properties and the Travelers Insurance Group acquired the Chrysler Center for approximately **$220 million**, negotiating a **150-year ground lease** with Cooper Union. Tishman spent a further $100 million on renovations.

---

### 2001 — 75% stake sold to TMW (German fund): $300 million

A 75% stake was sold to TMW, the German arm of an Atlanta-based investment fund managed by Prudential Financial, for **$300 million**.

---

### 2008 — Abu Dhabi Investment Council acquires 90% stake: ~$800 million

The **Abu Dhabi Investment Council (ADIC)**, a UAE sovereign wealth fund, purchased a **90% stake** for **~$800 million** — buying out TMW's 75% and Tishman Speyer's additional 15%. Tishman retained 10% and continued to manage the building. ADIC later merged into **Mubadala Investment Co.** in 2018.

> The $800 million figure derives from a single anonymous source cited in contemporaneous Bloomberg and WSJ reporting. Neither ADIC nor Tishman Speyer confirmed the price publicly. It is a reasonable working estimate; whether it was $750 million or $850 million, the conclusion is the same.

> Initial reporting described the transaction as a 75% stake (the TMW portion). The confirmed final structure was 90%, with Tishman retaining 10%.

---

### 2019 — Sold to RFR Holding and Signa: ~$151 million

After listing in January 2019, the leasehold was sold to **Aby Rosen's RFR Holding** and **Austrian developer Signa Holding** for approximately **$151 million** — an 81% loss on the 2008 price. Cooper Union simultaneously raised annual ground rent from $7.75 million to $32.5 million.

---

### 2023–2025 — Insolvency, lease termination, re-listed

- **2023:** Signa filed for insolvency; an Austrian court ordered it to sell its stake.
- **September 2024:** RFR defaulted on over $21 million in unpaid rent. Cooper Union terminated the lease and took control, hiring Cushman & Wakefield to manage the building.
- **Early 2025:** Cooper Union engaged Savills to find a new operator.
- **May 2025:** Building re-listed for sale. No asking price disclosed.

## The End Game — *R&S Chrysler LLC v. Cooper Union*

**Court:** Supreme Court, New York County — Commercial Division | **Date:** October 31, 2024
**Judge:** Hon. Jennifer G. Schecter | **Index No.:** 158973/2024 | **NYSCEF Doc. No.:** 73

Attached below is a summary of the actual judgment. Links to source documents are in the references. A warning: getting court case data can be a pain to nail down. Key exhibits are included to save the search.

### Result

Cooper Union prevailed on all points. Judge Schecter granted the motion for a Preliminary Injunction in full, directing subtenants to pay rent directly to Cooper Union and barring RFR from interfering with building operations — effective November 1, 2024.

### Background

RFR (operating as R&S Chrysler LLC) held the ground lease from Cooper Union. After accumulating $21 million in unpaid rent, Cooper Union terminated the lease on September 27, 2024. RFR filed suit seeking to block the takeover; Cooper Union counter-moved for injunctive relief.

### Orders Issued

- RFR enjoined from interfering with Cooper Union's **rent collection** from subtenants
- RFR enjoined from issuing any **default or termination notices** to subtenants
- RFR enjoined from interfering with Cooper Union's **management and operations**
- Cooper Union to **notify all subtenants** of new rent remittance instructions
- Cooper Union to **pay all building operating expenses** from November 1, 2024
- Cooper Union to post a **$500,000 undertaking** by November 14, 2024
- Judge encouraged both parties to **pursue settlement**

### RFR's Arguments & Dispositions

| Argument | Disposition |
|---|---|
| Cooper Union's handling of campus protests drove away tenants, excusing non-payment | **Rejected** — no case law supports withholding rent due to a landlord's off-premises conduct |
| Termination notice sent to wrong address | **Rejected** — actual receipt was undisputed; *"the flimsiest of flimsy"* |
| Injunction would grant Cooper Union ultimate relief prematurely | **Rejected** — rent collection rights are distinct from physical possession |
| Proposed escrow alternative | **Rejected** — lease Section 14.3 gave Cooper Union a direct contractual right to collect upon termination |

### Key Quotes

> *"In terms of any defenses, I haven't heard any that have merit."*

> *"This is not just a likelihood of success on the merits but almost certainty of success on the merits."*

> *"[The wrong address argument] seems like the flimsiest of flimsy."*

> *"There's not a case for anytime an owner of the building takes an unpopular position... based on a landlord's completely unrelated conduct, you have a right to withhold rent."*

— **Hon. Jennifer G. Schecter**

### Key Facts on Record

- RFR admitted to running a **deficit of ~$1.75M/month**, projected to worsen
- RFR had accumulated **$10–13M in arrears to vendors** concurrently with non-payment to Cooper Union
- RFR described by Cooper Union's counsel as a **judgment-proof shell company** with the Chrysler Building as its sole asset
- RFR sought **no injunction to forestall termination** before the cure period expired — a decisive procedural failure

## Root Cause

> "Good from afar, but far from good."

This sums up the basic intellectual failure of diligence. These are rookie errors made by people who let the desire to own an iconic asset overwhelm the need for basic analysis. As with many such cases, it belongs as much in a psychology post as in a credit or economics one.

What was bought was a leasehold in a building of diminishing competitive value. It was reasonable to expect — in real, post-capex terms — that cash flows from leasing would decline over time. The real killer was the ground lease. As New York investors slowly came to understand, separating buildings from land can increase total asset values. But only if you own the land. The appreciating asset was the land; the depreciating asset was the structure sitting on top of it.

Ground rent escalation, which building owners cannot control indefinitely, absorbed an increasing share of value over time. The $21 million in court-claimed arrears is not the cause — it is the symptom. The cause was the inability of third-party tenant rents to cover the ground rent.

There is also an element of spite and short-termism at play. If ground rents are set such that the building is uneconomic, it is ultimately an act of self-harm: no rational investor will acquire the building at any price. The ground rent then becomes a proxy for latent development value — value that may take decades to realise. Just because you can extract maximum rent does not mean it serves long-run interests.

Buildings are expensive to maintain. Anecdotal commentary suggests the Empire State Building — the other iconic Art Deco giant — runs to something like $40 million per annum in operating costs. That is a substantial claim on economic rents before any ground lease payment is considered.

Based on the facts reasonably available at the time of the 2008 transaction: was the demise inevitable? Probably not. But was it reasonable to conclude that the absence of long-run contractually fixed ground lease terms could imperil the investment? Yes, clearly. The purchase should have been conditional on a fully agreed long-run land lease extension. In investing, unlike in war, the counterfactual is available — and here it is damning.

This is basic stuff. Buying at the peak of the real estate cycle without securing the ground lease was an unforgivable failure of diligence. They bought a dud.

## Key Takeaways

- Hard facts matter.
- Discounted cash flow rules.
- Don't be stupid — this is the main one, and it oddly seems to be the hardest.
- Smart buyers lose a lot of bids. That is how they stay smart.
- The way you make money is mostly by not losing your capital.
- The conflation of NOI with NCF in large-scale commercial property needs to be erased from your thinking.
- Skin in the game matters. People do stupid things with other people's money when they face no downside.
