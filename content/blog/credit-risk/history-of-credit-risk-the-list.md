*This is a draft. It is an index post — a working list of the major credit risk events this site intends to cover individually, in the same style as the Countrywide and subprime-crisis posts. Entries below are one or two paragraphs each; the plan is that most of them eventually become their own full post. Status tags in bold show what already exists on this site today.*

## Why this list, and why this framing

The two-part series on the 2007–08 crisis made a deliberate argument: what looked, from a distance, like a systemic accident was in fact a chain of individually explicable decisions — a lender that called its own product "toxic" in private while selling it as conservative in public, rating agencies with a fee structure that rewarded generosity, an insurer that sold protection it had not reserved capital to honour. None of that was bad luck. It was judgment, exercised badly, at every link in the chain.

That framing is the point of this list. Almost every major credit risk event of the last 150 years reduces to the same handful of failures repeating in different costumes: concentration risk mistaken for diversification, leverage mistaken for skill, a model or a rating trusted past the point the underlying data supported it, a control function with no real authority to say no, or outright fraud dressed up as normal business. The rare genuine exceptions — a true bolt-from-the-blue macro shock with no antecedent human error — are conspicuously hard to find on this list. Where a listed event leans more toward "the market moved and a bet went wrong" than "someone should have known better," that is flagged below rather than smoothed over.

**Sourcing.** The core of this list is drawn from International Banker's ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/), which is credited as the base source and worth reading in full for the broader context around each entry. It has been reordered chronologically, extended backward to the 1870s, and supplemented with Long-Term Capital Management and several corporate/institutional defaults not on the original list, each checked against primary or authoritative secondary sources — linked inline and collected in Sources at the end.

**Coverage note.** This was checked directly against this site's own `content/blog/credit-risk/` folder. [Enron: A Look Back](/blog/credit-risk/enron-a-look-back/) has been live since March 2024. Also already live, and not from the original International Banker list at all: [Mfs Collapse](/blog/credit-risk/mfs-collapse/) (a UK bridging-lender collapse, added below as #25) and [Experience Matters](/blog/credit-risk/experience-matters/), on the 2026 blow-up of Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness LP (added below as #26). The site also already carries several one-off credit-risk case studies that sit outside this list's chronological, "major named crisis" scope — the Gherkin, the Chrysler Building, Centro/New Plan Excel, WeWork, and a Spanish SPV's post-Lehman swap litigation — rounded up in **Further case studies on this blog**, below the numbered list.

A rough ordering note: the request behind this list was to start with the highest defaults, going back to the 1870s. The list below is ordered chronologically from 1873 rather than ranked by inflation-adjusted loss size, since a size-ranking across 150 years of currency, GDP, and accounting changes is a genuinely contestable exercise in itself and would obscure the chronological throughline connecting one crisis's aftermath to the next crisis's causes.

## The list

### 1. 1873 — Panic of 1873 (Jay Cooke & Co. / Northern Pacific Railway)
**Status: not yet covered.** Jay Cooke & Co., the country's leading investment bank and the underwriter that had financed the Union side of the Civil War, over-committed itself as both promoter and lender to the Northern Pacific Railway, a transcontinental line built well ahead of any demand that could service its debt. When European investors began dumping American railroad bonds in mid-1873, Cooke's firm — unable to refinance its own exposure to a railroad it had every incentive to keep talking up — collapsed on September 18, 1873. The New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days, at least 100 banks failed nationwide, and the ensuing depression ran until 1879.((Federal Reserve History, ["Banking Panics of the Gilded Age"](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/banking-panics-of-the-gilded-age).)) The judgment failure is a textbook conflict of interest: the bank that stood to profit from the railroad's bonds was also the bank deciding how much railroad debt was prudent to hold.

### 2. 1890 — The Barings Crisis
**Status: not yet covered.** Baring Brothers, then the most prestigious merchant bank in London, channelled roughly £140 million into Argentine investment between 1885 and 1890 and ended up holding £8.3 million in "toxic" Argentine paper — including a failed water-and-sewerage bond issue — against only £4 million of capital.((Bank Underground, ["Rescuing a SIFI, Halting a Panic: the Barings Crisis of 1890"](https://bankunderground.co.uk/2016/02/10/rescuing-a-sifi-halting-a-panic-the-barings-crisis-of-1890/); National Bureau of Economic Research, ["A Monetary and Financial Wreck: The Baring Crisis, 1890–91"](https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c8837/c8837.pdf).)) That is dangerous leverage on a concentrated, single-country emerging-market bet, chased for yield in a period of easy money — the same shape of mistake that recurs throughout this list under different labels. The Bank of England organised a rescue in November 1890 (a £7.5 million advance plus a £17.1 million private-bank guarantee syndicate) that is now regarded as an early template for "too big to fail" intervention.

### 3. 1907 — Panic of 1907
**Status: not yet covered.** A failed attempt to corner the copper market triggered a loss of confidence in the Knickerbocker Trust Company, and the resulting bank run spread across New York's trust-company sector, wiping out nearly half the value of the NYSE before J.P. Morgan personally organised a private-sector rescue.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) The panic is usually told as a story about a cornering scheme gone wrong, but the deeper judgment failure is structural: trust companies had grown to rival national banks in size while sitting outside the reserve requirements and lender-of-last-resort access that were supposed to prevent exactly this kind of run — a regulatory-arbitrage pattern that reappears almost verbatim in the 2007–08 "shadow banking" system.

### 4. 1920 — The Ponzi Scheme
**Status: not yet covered.** Charles Ponzi promised 50% returns in 45 days from arbitraging international postal reply coupons, a trade that was real but nowhere near large enough to generate the returns he promised — so he paid early investors with money from new ones, the fraud that now bears his name.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) Less a credit risk event than the archetype for several later ones on this list (Madoff, and arguably FTX), it earns a place here as the template: a plausible-sounding strategy, a charismatic promoter, and investors who declined to ask why the returns were so much better than everyone else's.

### 5. 1929 — Wall Street Crash / "Black Monday"
**Status: not yet covered.** Years of speculative buying on margin — often with as little as 10% equity behind a stock position — met overproduction and an overvalued market; the Dow fell 12.82% on October 28, 1929, and roughly 89% from peak to trough over the following three years, precipitating the Great Depression.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) The judgment failure here is leverage without margin discipline at a market-wide scale — individually rational bets on continued appreciation that, in aggregate, left the system with no capacity to absorb a reversal.

### 6. 1970 — Bankruptcy of Penn Central
**Status: not yet covered.** The 1968 merger of the Pennsylvania Railroad and New York Central combined two rivals with incompatible management and near-total route duplication — one retrospective compared it to "a late-in-life marriage to which each partner brings a house, a summer cottage, two cars... plus car payments and mortgages on the houses."((Wikipedia, ["Bankruptcy of Penn Central"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bankruptcy_of_Penn_Central).)) Losses accelerated from $2.8 million to nearly $83 million a year within the decade before the company filed what was then the largest bankruptcy in American history, on June 21, 1970 — a $326 million collapse ($2.03 billion in 2024 dollars) that caught commercial-paper investors who had treated a household-name railroad's short-term debt as effectively risk-free.((Goldman Sachs, ["1970: Penn Central Bankruptcy"](https://www.goldmansachs.com/our-firm/history/moments/1970-penn-central-bankruptcy).)) The failure is a case study in a merger driven by rivalry and empire-building rather than operational logic, dressed up in accounting that obscured how bad things had become until it was too late.

### 7. 1974 — Bankhaus Herstatt
**Status: not yet covered.** A single trader at the small Cologne bank Herstatt ran up unauthorised foreign-exchange positions betting on continued Deutsche mark strength; when the dollar rallied instead, losses reached roughly five times the bank's entire capital. German regulators shut Herstatt down at 3:30pm Frankfurt time on June 26, 1974 — after European counterparties had already sent their Deutsche mark leg of the day's FX trades, but before New York had sent the matching dollars back. American banks lost around $328 million (roughly $2 billion today), Chase Manhattan alone absorbing $156 million.((Market Histories, ["The Herstatt Bank Failure: How One Afternoon in Cologne Created Settlement Risk"](https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-herstatt-bank-failure-how-one-afternoon-in-cologne-created-settlement-risk-1974).)) Beyond the initial judgment failure — one trader, essentially unsupervised, betting several times the bank's capital on a currency view — Herstatt is really the story of a second, structural failure: nobody had designed FX settlement to survive a counterparty going bankrupt mid-settlement. It directly produced the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision later that year, and the industry term "Herstatt risk."

### 8. 1984 — Continental Illinois National Bank
**Status: not yet covered.** Continental Illinois, then the seventh-largest US bank with roughly $40 billion in assets, bought large volumes of energy loans originated by the much smaller Penn Square Bank without doing its own underwriting — a Continental loan officer, John Lytle, later pleaded guilty to taking kickbacks for pushing bad loans through.((Wikipedia, ["Continental Illinois"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_Illinois).)) When Penn Square failed in 1982 and the energy sector turned down, depositors pulled over $10 billion from Continental in a matter of days in May 1984; the FDIC, the Fed, and a banking consortium combined for a rescue exceeding $18 billion, in a decision regulators explicitly justified on "too big to fail" grounds — the phrase's origin point. The judgment failure is buying underwriting quality secondhand, sight-unseen, because the loans were profitable and a smaller bank had already done the (inadequate) diligence.

### 9. 1989 — Savings and Loan Crisis
**Status: not yet covered.** Rising interest rates in the late 1970s and 1980s turned S&Ls' core business — long-term fixed-rate mortgages funded by short-term deposits — structurally unprofitable, and deregulation let many institutions respond by chasing yield in commercial real estate and junk bonds instead of shrinking. Over a thousand thrifts failed, and taxpayers ultimately funded a multi-hundred-billion-dollar resolution.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) A close cousin of the interest-rate mismatch that would fell Silicon Valley Bank 34 years later.

### 10. 1989–92 — Japanese Asset Price Bubble
**Status: not yet covered.** This one is easy to file under "macro event" and move on, but the underlying failure was as much a corporate valuation problem as a national one. Japanese banks lent freely against land as collateral — ordinary salarymen were extended loans of ¥100 million secured by their own homes — and corporate cross-shareholding between banks and their borrowers rose to roughly 67%, letting companies mark their balance sheets to inflated share prices rather than actual earnings.((Wikipedia, ["Japanese asset price bubble"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble).)) The result, at the peak, is one of the clearest illustrations on this entire list of a market collectively agreeing to believe an impossible number: the 1.15 km² grounds of the Tokyo Imperial Palace were estimated to be worth more than the entire real estate value of the state of California.((Wikipedia, ["Japanese asset price bubble"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_asset_price_bubble).)) Tokyo commercial land prices rose 42% in 1985 alone and a further 122% in 1986; the collapse began in early 1992 and fed into decades of "zombie bank" forbearance — banks and regulators alike declining to recognise loan losses rather than face them. Worth its own post for that forbearance angle as much as for the bubble itself: a slow-motion judgment failure stretched over twenty years, not a single bad decision.

### 11. 1997 — Asian Financial Crisis
**Status: not yet covered.** Thailand, Indonesia, South Korea, and their neighbours had borrowed heavily in dollars to fund growth while pegging their currencies to the dollar; when confidence broke, the pegs became indefensible and currency devaluation turned dollar debts unpayable overnight.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) The judgment failure sits on both sides — borrowers and lenders alike treated a currency peg as a permanent feature rather than a policy choice that could be abandoned under pressure.

### 12. 1998 — Long-Term Capital Management
**Status: not yet covered.** LTCM's partners included two Nobel laureates and ran a "market-neutral" arbitrage strategy that was, by the numbers, working — until it wasn't. By the end of 1997 the fund carried roughly $30 of debt for every $1 of capital, and rather than trim that leverage it returned capital to outside investors, concentrating the same bets over a smaller equity base.((Federal Reserve History, ["Near Failure of Long-Term Capital Management"](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/ltcm-near-failure).)) When Russia devalued the rouble and defaulted on domestic debt in August 1998, the "convergence" trades LTCM had bet on diverged instead, and the fund lost 44% of its value in a single month. Because LTCM's positions were so large relative to the market, and because its counterparties included nearly every major Wall Street bank, the New York Fed convened (without committing public money) a rescue in which fourteen banks injected $3.625 billion for 90% of the fund, unwinding it in orderly fashion over the following year.((Federal Reserve History, ["Near Failure of Long-Term Capital Management"](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/ltcm-near-failure).)) This is arguably the cleanest case on the entire list of "the model was right about everything except how much leverage the model itself could safely justify" — a direct ancestor of the risk-management failures in 2007–08.

### 13. 2000 — Dotcom Bubble Bursts
**Status: not yet covered.** Internet-sector valuations detached from any plausible path to profitability; the NASDAQ fell roughly 75% over two and a half years starting March 2000, destroying trillions in paper wealth.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) More a market-valuation and governance story (analyst conflicts of interest, IPO allocation practices) than a pure credit event, but it belongs on the list for the incentive-design parallel with 2007–08: analysts and underwriters paid to be bullish, rating the same deals they were selling.

### 14. 2001 — Enron
**Status: covered.** See [Enron: A Look Back](/blog/credit-risk/enron-a-look-back/), live on this site since March 2024. Enron used mark-to-market accounting and off-balance-sheet special-purpose entities to book anticipated future profits immediately and hide mounting losses and debt from investors; the stock collapsed from around $90 to under $1 within a year, and the company's bankruptcy was, at the time, the largest in US corporate history.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) Also took down Arthur Andersen, its auditor — a reminder that a captured or complicit control function is often as load-bearing to these failures as the fraud itself.

### 15. 2002 — WorldCom
**Status: not yet covered.** WorldCom reclassified billions of dollars in ordinary operating expenses as capital expenditure, spreading costs over years instead of recognising them immediately, to manufacture profits the underlying business was not generating; the resulting bankruptcy exceeded even Enron's in size.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) Worth pairing with Enron as a two-post treatment of the same accounting-era failure mode, in the same way the subprime crisis got a two-part treatment.

### 16. 2003 — Parmalat
**Status: not yet covered — corporate default, not on the original source list.** The Italian dairy multinational forged a Bank of America account statement claiming to hold €3.95 billion that did not exist, to disguise a decade of losses and looting by the founding Tanzi family; when the fraud collapsed in December 2003 it became, at the time, the largest corporate bankruptcy in European history and was widely labelled "Europe's Enron." Sits well alongside the Enron/WorldCom pair as the third leg of the early-2000s accounting-fraud cluster, and adds a useful non-US, non-financial-sector data point to the pattern.

### 17. 2007–08 — Subprime Mortgage Crisis / Global Financial Crisis
**Status: not yet covered on the live site (drafted separately).** A two-part series — behavioural and incentive failures inside Countrywide Financial, then how those failures were transmitted through securitisation, the rating agencies, AIG, and the repo market into a global crisis, including Northern Rock as the UK case study — exists in draft, pending a defamation/fairness pass, but is not yet published here.

### 18. 2008 — Volkswagen–Porsche Short Squeeze
**Status: not yet covered — flagged as the one entry on this list that is only loosely a "judgment failure" story.** Porsche's quiet accumulation of roughly 75% of Volkswagen's shares (via options as well as direct holdings) left short sellers needing to buy shares that were not really available once the position was disclosed in October 2008, briefly making VW the world's most valuable company by market capitalisation.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) This is closer to a cornering/market-structure event than a credit risk failure in the sense the rest of this list uses the term — the "failure of judgment" here belongs mainly to short sellers who assumed a stock's free float was larger than it actually was.

### 19. 2008 — Bernard Madoff
**Status: not yet covered.** Madoff ran the largest Ponzi scheme on record — reported client balances around $65 billion, of which perhaps $18–20 billion was actual invested principal — for decades, reporting steady positive returns that his real trading never generated, until the 2008 crisis drove enough redemption requests that there was no new money left to pay them.((International Banker, ["A Brief History of Financial Crises"](https://internationalbanker.com/history-of-financial-crises/history-of-financial-crises/).)) The judgment failure is distributed widely here: feeder funds, auditors, and a regulator (the SEC) that received specific, credible written warnings from Harry Markopolos years before the collapse and did not act on them.

### 20. 2011 — MF Global
**Status: not yet covered — corporate default, not on the original source list.** Former Goldman Sachs CEO and New Jersey governor Jon Corzine, running the brokerage MF Global, built a $6.3 billion "repo-to-maturity" bet on distressed European sovereign debt over the board's own reservations. When the position became public and creditors demanded collateral, the firm collapsed within days, filing for bankruptcy on October 31, 2011 — and roughly $1.6 billion in customer funds that should have been held segregated went missing in the scramble, eventually mostly recovered but in clear violation of commodity-law segregation rules.((Seven Pillars Institute, ["The MF Global Bankruptcy & Jon Corzine"](https://sevenpillarsinstitute.org/case-studies/jon-corzine-and-mf-global/).)) No criminal charges were ever filed against Corzine; he settled CFTC civil charges in January 2017, paying a $5 million personal penalty and accepting a ban from registering with the CFTC or associating with a futures commission merchant.((U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission, [press release 7508-17](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/7508-17).)) A near-textbook repeat of the Barings pattern from 1890: a concentrated, high-conviction bet on sovereign paper, oversized relative to the firm's capital, pushed through against internal caution. (Worth flagging: this is not the same firm as "MFS" / Market Financial Solutions, entry #25 below — similar-sounding name, unrelated company, fifteen years apart.)

### 21. 2020 — Wirecard
**Status: not yet covered.** The German payments company fabricated roughly €1.9 billion in cash balances that its long-serving auditor, EY, never independently confirmed existed, alongside forged partner contracts and round-tripped funds used to inflate reported profit for years. The fraud unravelled and the company filed for insolvency on June 25, 2020; CEO Markus Braun was arrested days later.((Wikipedia, ["Wirecard scandal"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wirecard_scandal).)) Braun's trial in Munich, alongside two co-defendants, began in December 2022 and — as of this draft — is still ongoing with no verdict yet delivered, so this needs describing as allegations still before the court, not settled fact, until a verdict lands.

### 22. 2022 — FTX
**Status: not yet covered.** The cryptocurrency exchange FTX and its affiliated trading firm Alameda Research were run by overlapping leadership with no effective wall between them; Alameda borrowed and spent more than half of FTX's customer deposits on illiquid investments and losses, leaving an $8 billion hole that surfaced when a liquidity crunch triggered a wave of withdrawal requests FTX could not meet. Both entities filed for bankruptcy on November 11, 2022.((Wikipedia, ["Bankruptcy of FTX"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bankruptcy_of_FTX).)) FTX's own restructuring CEO, John J. Ray III — who had also overseen the Enron wind-down — described what he found as "a complete failure of corporate controls." Founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in November 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in prison in March 2024. A useful bookend to this list: the same absence of segregated-funds discipline that undid MF Global in 2011, at a much larger scale and with intent rather than a bad bet at its centre.

### 23. 2023 — Silicon Valley Bank
**Status: not yet covered.** SVB — at $209 billion in assets, the 16th-largest US bank — had parked $91.3 billion in long-duration Treasury and agency bonds without meaningfully hedging interest-rate risk, and funded itself with a deposit base that was roughly 89% uninsured and heavily concentrated in tech-sector startups whose deposit balances moved in lockstep with the same venture-funding cycle. The bank's chief risk officer role sat vacant for most of 2022, precisely as the Fed's rate hikes were accumulating over $15 billion in unrealised losses on the bond book.((Wikipedia, ["Collapse of Silicon Valley Bank"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_Silicon_Valley_Bank).)) When SVB announced a loss-crystallising asset sale on March 8, 2023, depositors — coordinating partly over group chats and Slack channels among the same venture-capital networks that had concentrated the deposit base in the first place — pulled $42 billion in a single day; regulators seized the bank on March 10. A clean modern echo of the savings-and-loan mismatch from 1989, with social media replacing the physical bank queue.

### 24. 2023 — Credit Suisse
**Status: not yet covered.** Credit Suisse's collapse was less a single event than the final act of a multi-year pattern: a $4.7 billion loss from prime-brokerage exposure to the family office Archegos Capital Management in April 2021, followed within months by the collapse of $10 billion in supply-chain finance funds tied to Greensill Capital (roughly $3 billion of investor losses), each traced by post-mortems to inadequate risk-management sign-off on concentrated counterparty exposure.((Wikipedia, ["Credit Suisse"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Credit_Suisse).)) Those losses, a CHF 7.3 billion annual loss reported in February 2023, and a run of "material weakness" disclosures left the 166-year-old bank with no capital cushion of confidence left when March 2023's US regional-bank turmoil (see Silicon Valley Bank, above) hit an already-nervous market; UBS agreed to acquire Credit Suisse for $3.25 billion in a government-brokered, all-stock deal announced March 19, 2023.((Bloomberg, ["Credit Suisse–UBS Takeover: How a 166-Year-Old Bank Collapsed"](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2023-03-20/credit-suisse-ubs-takeover-how-a-166-year-old-bank-collapsed).)) The judgment failure repeats across both 2021 scandals: large, concentrated single-counterparty exposures approved without the risk function apparently having real veto power.

### 25. 2026 — Market Financial Solutions (MFS) Collapse
**Status: covered.** See [Mfs Collapse](/blog/credit-risk/mfs-collapse/), live on this site. Not from the original International Banker list — added here because it's already covered, still unfolding, and fits this list's pattern almost too neatly. Market Financial Solutions, a Mayfair-based bridging and buy-to-let lender with a claimed £2.4 billion loan book, entered administration on 25 February 2026 after a High Court judge cited "very serious" allegations of fraud and double-pledging — the same properties used as collateral for multiple loans from different lenders, undisclosed. Administrators could verify only around £230 million of collateral against debts exceeding £1.16 billion. Barclays (~£500m exposure, later booking a £228m impairment), Apollo/Atlas SP, HSBC (~£293m direct, plus a further ~$400m indirect loss disclosed through a structured back-leverage arrangement routed through Apollo), Elliott, and Jefferies were all exposed. MFS's own accountant was a two-person firm operating from a basement office near Victoria Coach Station, whose owner also held personal directorships in more than 130 companies that had themselves borrowed from MFS — a conflict of interest that sat underneath a "clean" unqualified audit opinion as recently as March 2025. As of this draft, litigation and an FCA enforcement investigation are ongoing and no findings of fact have been made; the founder denies intentional fraud through his legal representatives.

### 26. 2026 — Situational Awareness LP
**Status: covered.** See [Experience Matters](/blog/credit-risk/experience-matters/), live on this site. Also not from the original list, and smaller in absolute terms than most entries above, but a clean illustration of the same mechanism playing out in a single fund. Situational Awareness LP, built around AI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner's widely read 2024 essay of the same name, scaled to roughly $45 billion in assets under management by mid-2026 on returns exceeding 1,000% since inception. Over roughly three weeks in July 2026, a concentrated, leveraged, single-thesis position gave back around $35 billion of that, forcing a distressed sale of its public equity book to Citadel and shrinking the fund to around $10 billion, mostly held in a single illiquid private stake exempt from margin calls. The judgment failure isn't about whether the underlying AI-acceleration thesis was right — it's the one running through Barings (1890), LTCM (1998), and MF Global (2011): a high-conviction, concentrated, leveraged bet where "the thesis is correct" and "the position survives long enough for that to matter" turn out to be two different questions.
