## Overview

There is a particular kind of financial disaster where everything at the bottom of the structure keeps working exactly as intended, and everything at the top falls apart anyway. Centro Properties Group is a good example of this

Centro, an experienced owner operator of commercial real estate assets, acquired New Plan Excel Realty Trust ("NXL") in 2007 for approximately US$6.2 billion. NXL owned a large portfolio of grocery-anchored US shopping centres — high occupancy, stable rents, conservative leverage, the kind of assets that were supposed to be boring. They were boring. They kept paying their mortgages throughout the crisis. The problem was that the cash they generated could not get from where it was to where it needed to be.

This post is specifically about the US debt lockup — the mechanism by which structurally subordinated cash flows became unavailable to Centro's Australian parent entities, and why the consolidated financial statements published in the 2007 Annual Report obscured this until it was too late.

Before we proceed, context for those who did not experience this in person. Unlike today (2026 at the time of writing), the economy worked. There was growth, things were booming. Inflation was dead (joke on us there), if you bothered going to university you got good job offer, you could see a doctor, cars were cheap, rent was tolerable, you could afford a house, etc, etc. In short life was good. No one on the acquisition path was adopting a conservative mindset. Stress, or fragility analysis as I prefer to call it was a moot point. Debt was available in abundance.

Those were the days. The context matters for what came next. Centro management was in the end hoisted by their own petard as such, but it was not malicious in my view. NXL was not an irrational acquisition. Unlike some others, the intent here was not to deceive investors and hide an unsustainable debt stack. Centro screwed up their structure, sure, but mostly they ran into a once in a generation environment where what always goes up, commercial real estate, went down. Who knew, hello 1990's, you were not missed. To give you an idea I was on calls with CMBS Special Servicers, and bids on brand new regional commercial real estate was in the order of 35c on the dollar. There were so many defaults, that the Specials Servicers just left a message saying call us back when there is a cash default, we are too busy to deal with things that have not tipped over yet. To opine that this was not normal is an understatement. But that was after, at the time of the acquisition all was well in world, and risk on appetite ruled the day.

## A Brief History of Centro Before the Fall

Centro was not a fly-by-night operation. It was established on 18 February 1985 as Jennings Properties Limited, listed on the Australian Stock Exchange the same year, and renamed Centro Properties Limited in January 1991. It grew steadily through the 1990s as a domestic retail property manager before restructuring into a stapled security in 1997 and expanding internationally through the 2000s. They were not terrible at their job, and had good assets on their balance sheet.

By the time of the NXL acquisition, Centro had been delivering compound annual distribution growth of 7.6% per annum over ten years and was the highest-performing listed property trust on the ASX over that period. The 2007 Annual Report was not misleading about this history — it was genuinely an impressive operational track record. Andrew Scott had been CEO since 1997. The board had deep property and financial experience. This was not a management team that stumbled into the business; it was one that had built it carefully over two decades and then, in a single transaction, bet the architecture of the entire structure on continuous access to cheap credit markets.

That is what makes it instructive rather than simply cautionary. The operational business was sound. The long-term earnings record was real. The collapse had nothing to do with any of that. The end was self inflicted through the borrowing structure. The macro challenges were not the fault of Centro, their fragility to leverage was rather self inflicted though.

The (over) simplified structure is:

| Entity | ASX Code | Primary Function |
|----------|----------|------------------|
| Centro Properties Group | CNP | Parent management and investment vehicle. Owned strategic property interests, earned management fees, undertook developments and redevelopments, and sponsored property funds. |
| Centro Retail Trust | CER | Retail property trust focused on owning income-producing shopping centres and distributing rental income to investors. |

## Centro Going Into the Acquisition

The 2007 Annual Report is a period document worth reading with the benefit of hindsight. The headline numbers look strong. Funds under management grew 131% to A$26.6 billion. Distributable earnings rose 13.5% to A$335.3 million. Distributions per security increased 8.2% to 39.8 cents. Total investor return was 46.0% for the year. The Chairman described it as "another strong financial year."

What the annual report also shows, if you look at the balance sheet rather than the income statement, is that this growth was entirely debt funded. Total borrowings moved from A$1.537 billion to A$3.604 billion — an increase of 134.5% in a single year. Total assets grew 57.9% to A$8.137 billion. Gearing on a lenders look-through basis rose to 48.4%, above Centro's own stated preferred range of 35%–45%.

Interest cover ratio fell from 4.9x to 2.8x against a stated policy floor of 2.0x. Still above the policy minimum — but the direction of travel was significant and the headroom was thinning.

The management commentary acknowledged the gearing overshoot, noting that Centro was "confident that its gearing level will be restored to within its policy range during the 2008 financial year primarily through continued strong inflows into Centro's managed funds." That confidence would prove misplaced when credit markets closed in late 2007.

Now a note of caution here. REITS relied on increases in values to drive increases in leverage, and paid out the gap in dividends heavily subsidised by debt, not so much cash yields. Nothing like a directors valuation uplift to make your balance sheet look good. At some point the party stopped, the wall of free debt left the room, suddenly you had to earn your living in cash. No one was ready. Well, not no one, but many got caught.

## REIT Primer

REITs are Real Estate Investment Trusts. They are often confused in nature with the corporate entities that they appear to behave as.

> REITs are trusts in name and nature. Trusts as legal vehicles are a centuries old concept. The concept originated in medieval England, where landowners leaving for the Crusades transferred legal ownership of land to trusted individuals who managed it on behalf of the original owner or their family. Over time, English courts developed the distinction between legal ownership (held by the trustee) and beneficial ownership (held by beneficiaries), creating modern trust law. Of note they were never intended to be, or to carry leverage. REITs pay out circa 90% of earnings, so retain very little liquidity for a rainy day. This means fragility is a design feature, and care is needed when lending to REITs.

## New Plan Excel: The Asset They Bought

> On 27 August 2007, Centro Retail Trust (“CER”) announced its proposal to merge with Centro Shopping America Trust (“CSF”). Under the proposed merger, Centro agreed to sell approximately $2.2 billion of Australasian and US property assets in return for units in the newly merged entity. This merger proposal is subject to investor approval in October 2007, at which time the assets would be transferred from Centro. The transfer is expected to have a minimal impact on Centro’s earnings and balance sheet position.

New Plan Excel was in good shape before Centro acquired it. For FY2006 the key metrics were:

| Metric | FY2006 |
|--------|--------|
| Occupancy | 93.6% |
| Net Operating Income | ~US$300m |
| Funds From Operations | ~US$201m |
| Debt / Undepreciated Book Value | 46.3% |
| Average Debt Maturity | 5.6 years |
| Fixed Rate Debt | 86% |

The portfolio consisted primarily of grocery-anchored neighbourhood and community centres across the United States. Kroger, TJX Companies, Wal-Mart, Supervalu and Safeway were among the top tenants. This was necessity-based, non-discretionary retail — the kind of portfolio that holds occupancy through recessions because people still need supermarkets.

NXL was not distressed. It was not a turnaround. It was a stable, cash-generative, conventionally financed US REIT. The crisis that followed had nothing to do with the underlying properties failing to perform. They kept performing under arduous economic circumstances. So it met the purpose test as an acquisition, the asset level risk profile met expectations in the main. That is the central point. It's also why everyone largely stopped using their brain. Vanilla assets, move on, nothing to see here.

## The Acquisition Structure and What It Created

Centro financed the NXL acquisition using bridge facilities, intending to refinance into permanent capital markets in due course. This was standard practice for large acquisitions in 2007. The problem was that the bridge came due, credit markets froze, and the refinancing never happened. 

On 20 April 2007, Centro Super LLC (jointly owned 50/50 by CNP and CER) acquired 100% of the issued share capital of the New Plan Excel Realty Property Trust (‘New Plan’).

### How the New Plan Excel Acquisition Was Financed

The New Plan Excel (NXL) acquisition in 2007 was financed through a combination of:

1. Assumed NXL debt and preferred securities

2. New acquisition debt raised by Centro

3. Large equity raisings by CNP and CER

4. Bridge financing intended to be refinanced later

The financing structure is important because it directly contributed to Centro's later liquidity crisis. The gory details are laid out in full in the court judgement, link to original below. It's worth a read for the accounting treatment commentary. Let this be a moral lesson for us all, be nice in your work emails, they might be subject to discovery and published.

### Transaction Overview

| Item | Approx. Amount (US$bn) |
|--------|--------:|
| Equity value paid to NXL shareholders | ~3.7 |
| Assumed debt and preferred stock | ~2.5 |
| Total transaction value | ~6.2 |

Centro did not write a US$6.2 billion cheque. A large portion consisted of debt already sitting inside NXL that Centro assumed at closing.

### Step 1: Equity Raising

To fund the cash purchase price, Centro raised substantial equity from investors.

| Vehicle | Equity Raised |
|----------|------------:|
| CER | ~A$1.3bn |
| CNP | ~A$1.8bn |
| Total | ~A$3.1bn |

These were among the largest Australian REIT equity raisings at the time. The market initially supported them because Centro had a long record of successful acquisitions. By which I mean Centro as a habitual capital raiser paid a lot of ECM and DCM fees. But in truth, to be fair, they had expanded for 25 years without problems to date.

### Step 2: Acquisition Debt

The remaining funding came from a large syndicated acquisition facility provided by a group of international banks.

The facility was effectively a bridge between acquisition and permanent financing.

Each layer of this structure had its own creditors, its own security package, and its own claim on cash flows before anything could be distributed upward. This is the structure that made the consolidated accounts so misleading.

Some context. There was a wall of debt available. Then there wasn't. Early days spreads widening. The smart management bit the bullet and progressively rolled into higher credit spreads. Their reckoning was rolling debt to survive was essential. They were right. Many hung on, outraged at market spreads. They waited for spread compression. It didn't come. They waited more. Spreads really widened at the height of the crisis. Then became usurious on anything other than top tier assets. When you don't roll your debt stack as a REIT, when you carry 70% leverage, you are playing with fire. Those who went early survived. Those who waited suffered.

## The Consolidated View vs. the Legal Entity View

The 2007 Annual Report shows Centro's consolidated interest cover at 2.8x and distributable earnings of A$335.3 million. These numbers reflect the aggregated financial performance of the entire corporate structure treated as a single entity. Lovely!

This is the wrong frame for credit analysis. The relevant question is not whether the consolidated group generates enough income to service consolidated debt. The relevant question is whether each legal entity generates enough income to service *its own* debt obligations, *after* paying the creditors that rank above it.

When you deconsolidate and apply that lens, a different picture emerges:

**Property level:** The individual shopping centres were generating stable NOI and servicing their property mortgages without stress. Occupancy across the US portfolio was 94.8% at June 2007. Comparable property income growth was 2.1%. Nothing at this level was failing. Even as values were a bit pie in the sky in terms of cap rates.

**Operating partnership level:** Excel Realty Partners LP sat between the property subsidiaries and the US HoldCo entities. Distributions from the properties had to flow through the operating partnership, which carried its own debt obligations. Any covenant restrictions or cash trap provisions at this level reduced what was available to move upward.

> Always look at the structure. Deconsolidate into economic sub units, regional units, or asset level sub units, according to the nature of the business. Read the loan docs. If there are 10 assets, all subject to secured finance with lock up clauses, and no cross acceleration, there is really no such thing as consolidated anything. You need 10 ops models and 10 lots of debt service assessment for credit risk.

**US HoldCo level:** The US Lending Group (approximately US$1.3 billion) and the US Private Placement Notes (approximately US$450 million) were secured against equity pledges and ownership interests in the US entities. Approximately US$1.75 billion of debt at this level had first claim on distributions coming up from the operating partnership, before anything could reach Australia.

> Debt against equity. Refer Casino. This is the definition of wrong way risk. Also pay attention to domicile, US is remarkably debtor friendly. DIP laws surprise non US participants when the day arrives. What do you mean I have to *pay money to fund* my borrower under default scenario??? Que rookie lender outrage if you please.

**Australian parent level:** The Australian syndicated facilities (approximately A$3.0 billion) were structurally subordinated to everything below them. Their security was indirect — ultimately dependent on distributions flowing up through the entire US structure. When those distributions stopped, the Australian parent entities had no access to the US cash flows, regardless of what the consolidated income statement showed.

By the time credit markets closed in late 2007 and the bridge facilities came due, Centro had approximately A$3.9 billion of debt maturing within approximately two months. The cash to service it existed — it was sitting inside the US structure, generating cash every month from grocery-anchored retail rents. It just could not get out.

## The Debt Maturity Profile: The Proximate Cause

The balance sheet at 30 June 2007 shows the scale of what was coming due. Interest bearing liabilities classified as current — meaning due within 12 months — totalled A$1.097 billion. The management commentary and subsequent disclosures made clear that approximately A$3.9 billion of total maturities were due within a very short window post the balance sheet date.

Centro's management statement acknowledged this explicitly in the context of contingent liquidity mechanisms: Centro "maintains unused bank facilities and cash in excess of its commitments." By December 2007, this assessment had been overtaken by events. The credit market environment that had allowed continuous short-term refinancing of acquisition bridge facilities ceased to exist.

> Short term funding of what is long term underlying assets. Duration mismatch. Many, many borrowers and funds have died a death on this hill. Don't be stupid, term out your obligations.

The NXL assets themselves were not the problem. The problem was that Centro had acquired A$17.4 billion of US funds under management using short-term funding instruments, on the assumption that those instruments could be continuously refinanced. When that assumption failed, the Australian parent had approximately A$191 million of cash on its balance sheet against A$3.9 billion of near-term maturities.

> In essence, as at 30 June 2007, CNP’s current assets could only meet 41% of its current financial obligations. This had declined from the reported surplus positions of 182% and 273% as at 31 December 2006 and 30 June 2006 respectively.  the proportion of short-term debt funding (less than 12 months) increased from 0% as at 31 December 2006 to 72% as at 30 June 2007. That is, over a six month period, CNP’s funding position had changed from being entirely funded by medium to long-term finance (greater than 12 months ) to being mostly funded by short-term finance. Queue wider DCM market meltdown, right at the moment when fragility was at maximum. The DCM issuer trend, was no longer Centro's friend. With the debts covered by g'ees that lacked the ability to pay at top co. Ouch!

## Why Consolidated Accounts Obscured This

The 2007 Annual Report presents a consolidated income statement showing total revenue and income of A$795 million, financing costs of A$221 million, and net profit of A$470 million. By any conventional reading of these numbers, the group appeared to be generating substantial income well in excess of its interest obligations.

The problem is that these numbers aggregate income from entities at multiple levels of the structure without distinguishing between income that is accessible to the Australian parent and income that is structurally trapped inside subsidiary entities.

Consider what the consolidated income statement includes:
- Property ownership revenue: A$91 million (directly owned Centro properties)
- Property services revenue: A$184 million (management fees — not trapped, but dependent on fund structures continuing)
- Managed vehicle revenue: A$91 million (distributions from managed funds — dependent on those funds making distributions)
- Share of net profits of associates: A$318 million (equity-accounted, non-cash at parent level)
- Fair value movements: A$80 million (non-cash)

The A$318 million share of associate profits is the critical item. Under equity accounting, Centro recognised its proportionate share of the profits of its various associated entities and managed funds. This income appeared on the consolidated income statement. It did not represent cash flowing to the Australian parent. It represented Centro's notional share of profits being retained inside entities that had their own debt obligations, their own creditor claims, and their own cash management requirements.

> You can't pay cash interest due at top co with notional accruals of something you don't have. Ever.

The distributable earnings reconciliation in Note 2 adjusts for some of this — stripping out revaluations, fair value movements, and the non-distributable component of associate profits — but even the distributable earnings of A$335 million represent a group-level figure that obscures the legal entity cash flows.

A lender to the Australian parent entities needed to ask a different question: not "what does the consolidated group earn?" but *"what cash can the Australian parent actually receive?"* The answer to the second question was substantially smaller and substantially less certain than the consolidated numbers suggested.

## The DSCR Picture by Legal Entity Level

If you apply a debt service coverage analysis at each structural level rather than at the consolidated level, the picture changes materially:

| Structural Level | Condition at June 2007 | Assessment |
|-----------------|----------------------|------------|
| Individual property | Stable — 94.8% occupancy, positive NOI growth | Mortgage debt serviced without stress |
| Operating partnership | Moderate pressure from acquisition-related costs | Distributable cash reduced but positive |
| US HoldCo (US$1.75bn debt) | Significant refinancing risk | Dependent on refinancing markets |
| Australian parent (A$3.0bn+ debt) | Severe — near-term maturities, no direct asset access | Crisis condition |

The collapse did not propagate upward from the assets. It propagated downward from the funding structure. The Australian parent ran out of refinancing capacity while the underlying retail properties continued to collect rent.

## What This Means for Credit Analysis

Several lessons emerge from this structure that remain relevant.

**Asset quality is necessary but not sufficient.** NXL was a high quality portfolio. It did not save Centro. Cash flows that are trapped structurally below the entity you are lending to provide no coverage for your debt, regardless of what the consolidated income statement shows.

**Consolidated DSCR is the wrong metric for multi-entity structures.** Centro's consolidated interest cover was 2.8x at June 2007. This number was technically accurate and substantively misleading. The relevant metrics were legal entity level DSCRs, the proportion of consolidated income accessible to the Australian parent, and the refinancing profile of the bridge facilities. Technically correct is a task for accountants. 'What does the (correct) number *really mean*, and is it realistic?', is the credit analyst question. These are different thought modes.

**Refinancing risk compounds structural subordination.** The Australian parent's problem was not just that it was structurally subordinated — it was that its debt was short-term while the assets were long-lived. This mismatch meant that continuous access to refinancing markets was not a convenience but a survival condition. Any disruption to those markets would be immediately fatal.

**The direction of travel in financial ratios matters.** Interest cover falling from 4.9x to 2.8x in one year, gearing rising above stated policy limits, borrowings more than doubling — individually each of these could be explained away. In aggregate they described a balance sheet optimised for a world of continuously available cheap credit with no resilience against disruption.

The 2007 Annual Report was titled "Resilience for Growth." By December 2007, Centro had announced that it was unable to refinance its short-term debt facilities. The assets were still generating cash. The structure was not.

> This is why you don't take subject aspects of annual reports at face value. Facts, yes. Management inclination is never to tell you the problems, they want to imply what might not be realistic. You can be factually correct and still be entirely misleading on subject comments.

## What Happened Next

Centro announced in December 2007 that it was unable to refinance approximately A$3.9 billion of near-term debt maturities. The share price, which had traded above A$8 earlier in 2007, collapsed to cents. Centro was effectively taken over by its lenders, its ASX market capitalisation falling from A$7.2 billion to around A$37 million.

What followed was several years of painful restructuring. 

Eight Centro directors were subsequently found by the Federal Court to have breached their duties — specifically for failing to ensure the financial accounts correctly classified the short-term debt maturities as current liabilities. PricewaterhouseCoopers, the auditor, faced a separate class action. The accounting failure was real, but as commentators noted at the time, it was not the cause of the collapse. The cause was the leverage and the structure.

Maurice Blackburn and Slater & Gordon, brought a class-action lawsuit against the company in 2008 over the accounting and refinancing errors.

[Read the gory details here on Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/francinemckenna/2011/07/12/pwc-will-answer-for-centro-but-judge-slams-directors-first/)

> The coverage around junior PwC staff incorrectly attributing > $1bn of debt as long term. Whoops. When everyone relies on everyone else, no one is left thinking.

[Class action results summary here](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=175d0ca8-c50e-4dc4-bd8b-3eaef8830f2f)

> The litigation settled (of course it did). The settlement involved a payment of $200m (including costs). The Centro companies contributed $95m to the settlement amount with the balance being contributed by PWC ($67m) and insurance proceeds ($38m).

The US portfolio — including the NXL assets — was eventually sold. Those shopping centres passed through several ownership structures before ultimately forming part of what became Brixmor Property Group, now a major US retail REIT. The Australian properties were retained and restructured. Centro itself was renamed Federation Centres and subsequently merged to become Vicinity Centres, one of Australia's largest retail property groups, now traded on the ASX as VCX.

The irony is complete: the underlying businesses on both sides of the Pacific survived. The grocery-anchored US retail portfolio that Centro paid US$6.2 billion for continued generating stable income through the global financial crisis, exactly as the asset analysis would have predicted. The Australian shopping centres kept operating. It was the financing architecture that failed, not the real estate.

## Key Takeaways

The Centro/NXL case is useful precisely because the underlying assets did not fail. This makes it a clean study in structural credit failure rather than an operational failure. The shopping centres kept trading. The mortgages kept being serviced. The problem was entirely architectural.

If you are analysing a consolidated entity that owns assets through multiple layers of subsidiary structure, the consolidated income statement is a starting point, not an answer. The questions that matter are:

- Where in the structure is the cash generated? Deconsolidate to this!
- What creditors rank ahead of the entity you are lending to?
- What legal and contractual restrictions govern upward cash flow?
- What is the refinancing profile of the HoldCo debt, and under what market conditions would it fail?
- What is the cash position of the HoldCo in isolation, net of near-term maturities?
- It's a REIT so liquidity is always constrained.
- Valuations are opinions on what the asset probably would have sold for, in the past. It is a rear vision metric. It is not a guarantee, it is not worth the paper its printed on in a stressed market. The content of the valuation (the data) is invaluable. The number is best taken with a grain of salt. Owners are Borrowers. Borrowers want maximum debt, which requires maximum possible valuations. Owners select and pay valuers. Valuations are thus rarely ever conservative. Conservative valuers are unemployed valuers.

> Centro's 2007 consolidated accounts could not answer those questions. They were not designed to. That is why they were dangerous. But the question was answerable to anyone who was a half decent analyst and read the disclosures properly and used their brains.
