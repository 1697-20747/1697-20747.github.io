*This is a draft. It's a third companion to the two index posts ("The List" and "Simplified Timeline") — where those are narrative, this one is a data table: every year of S&P Global Ratings' published global corporate default count and dollar volume since the series begins in 1981, converted to both nominal and real (2024-dollar) terms.*

## Source and scope, read this before the table

The counts and dollar figures below come from S&P Global Ratings' *2024 Annual Global Corporate Default And Rating Transition Study*.((S&P Global Ratings, [2024 Annual Global Corporate Default And Rating Transition Study](https://maalot.co.il/Publications/FTS20250331162126.pdf), published via S&P Global's Israeli affiliate Maalot; S&P publishes this study annually and freely, and it's the standard reference dataset for this kind of long-run default history.)) A few scope points that matter before reading the numbers as "all corporate defaults, ever":

- **Rated universe only.** This counts issuers that were rated by S&P at some point before defaulting — overwhelmingly large, bond-market-financed companies. It excludes the much larger population of unrated small and mid-sized business failures, private-credit defaults, and bank-loan-only borrowers. The 1873 Panic and 1874's 16.3% default rate cited on the full list post come from a *different* dataset (Giesecke/Longstaff/Schaefer/Strebulaev's 150-year study, itself built from 19th-century railroad-bond records) — the two series aren't directly comparable and shouldn't be spliced together as one continuous line.
- **"Default" includes distressed exchanges.** S&P counts a distressed debt exchange (creditors accepting less than originally promised, under threat of a worse outcome) as a default, not just a formal bankruptcy filing. This matters a lot for reading the recent years below — see 2023–24 note further down.
- **Real-dollar conversion is mine, not S&P's.** S&P publishes nominal dollar figures; the "real (2024 $)" column applies the US CPI-U annual-average index to restate every year in 2024 dollars.((U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, [Historical CPI-U, all items, U.S. city average](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/tables/supplemental-files/historical-cpi-u-202412.pdf).)) That's a real methodological simplification worth flagging explicitly: the underlying defaulted debt is a *global* figure (US, European, and other issuers), and a US consumer price index is not a precise deflator for a global corporate-debt figure — a GDP deflator or a blended global price index would be more defensible for a published version of this table. Treated here as "close enough to show the shape of the pattern," not as a precision-grade real-terms series.

| 5 | **2016** — 163 | **2024** — $227.4B | **2016** — $313.4B |

2009 is the peak on every measure — no surprise, that's the GFC/subprime aftermath already covered by entries #8, #17, #19 on the full list (Continental Illinois's descendant "too big to fail" era, the subprime crisis itself, and Madoff, whose scheme unwound in the same liquidity crunch). What's more interesting is where the ranking *changes* between nominal and real terms: 2016 outranks 2002 in raw dollars ($239.8B vs $190.9B), but once both are restated in 2024 dollars, 2002 pulls back ahead ($332.9B vs $313.4B) — a reminder that comparing nominal dollar figures across a 14-year gap without adjusting for inflation quietly overstates how much bigger the more recent year actually was. 2016's default wave, for context, was concentrated in the US energy and metals/mining sectors during the 2014–16 oil price collapse — a commodity-price-driven cluster of debt taken on when oil was $100+ and serviced when it wasn't, rather than a single named institutional failure the way most of the numbered list is structured; it isn't its own entry on the full list for that reason but is a real candidate for one.

## 2023–24: a shape worth flagging

The most recent two years in the table (153 defaults in 2023, 145 in 2024) sit well above the 2010s norm despite neither year featuring a systemic crisis on the scale of 2008–09 or 2020. S&P's own commentary on the 2024 study attributes a majority of that year's defaults — 59.3% — to **distressed exchanges** rather than outright bankruptcy: companies persuading creditors to accept less than they were owed, under threat of a worse outcome, instead of filing.((S&P Global Ratings, [2024 Annual Global Corporate Default And Rating Transition Study](https://maalot.co.il/Publications/FTS20250331162126.pdf).)) That's a genuine shift in *how* corporate distress gets resolved, not just a count of failures — arguably a private-markets and leveraged-loan-documentation story (looser covenants making distressed exchanges easier to execute) rather than a repeat of the 2001 or 2008 pattern. It's also the same period this blog's own **MFS** and **Situational Awareness LP** entries sit in (both 2026), and the same Jamie Dimon "cockroach" quote used in the MFS post — "when you see one cockroach, there's probably more" — was made in the context of exactly this elevated-default backdrop.

## How this connects to the other two posts in this series

- **1874's 16.3% rate** (flagged on the full list's Panic-of-1873 entry) predates this S&P series by over a century and uses a different dataset and methodology — noted above, not blended into this table.
- **2001–02** (Enron, WorldCom — full list entries #14–15) is the second-largest real-dollar default cluster in this table, behind only 2008–09.
- **2008–09** (Subprime/GFC — full list entry #17, currently only drafted, not yet live) is the largest cluster on every measure in this table by a wide margin — $917.8B in 2024 dollars in 2009 alone, comfortably exceeding the *combined* real-dollar total of every other year in the 1990s.
- **2020** (not individually covered on the full list, though Wirecard, FTX, SVB and Credit Suisse bracket it chronologically) is the third-largest cluster, COVID-driven.
- **2023–24** ties directly to the [Mfs Collapse](/blog/credit-risk/mfs-collapse/) and [Experience Matters](/blog/credit-risk/experience-matters/) entries already live on this site.
