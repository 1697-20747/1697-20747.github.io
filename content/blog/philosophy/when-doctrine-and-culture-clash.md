## Overview

Have you ever witnessed, first hand, behaviour that was diametrically opposite to the stated culture of an organisation - often from senior staff who, moments earlier, were banging on about the merits of policy X? Have you seen the performative action that adds no long-term value taken over the deep-value decision that would simply take longer to show progress? Have you watched clear discrimination, or even sexual harassment, get covered up when the HR policy says no such outcome is tolerable?

I suspect you have. Just like me. Just like lots of people. Because the pursuit of individual power makes this inevitable.

Is the choice of language used more important than the topic or the outcome being discussed? Equality, anyone? Is the complex reduced to overly simplistic alignment, or orthodoxy? This essay picks at some of the underlying frameworks for why. Nothing new under the sun here, but perhaps it will shift your thinking. A few quick examples to set the scene:

- Epstein et al - how the mighty have fallen once their actual behaviour was laid bare.
- Church scandals. Pick your favourite, there are too many to choose from. Pick your religion too - it's the same on every side of the fence, Christian, Muslim, Buddhist, whatever.
- The military - sexual abuse scandals, et al.
- Corporate fraud. Endless choice there.
- Pick a random cult.
- Governments, local and federal.
- The rise of cancel culture on the left - now there's a moral contradiction.
- People-first-anything, when the actual treatment of staff is the inverse.
- Scientology, though down to one famous member now (Tom Cruise), so less relevant.

This applies to groups of any kind, but given the literature and people's actual experience, we'll stick to the usual subjects - corporations and larger organisations. The difference between them is minimal.

The question worth answering is why people behave so differently from the culture they claim to hold. It is far more complex than wilful disobedience. It's closer to the old line, "do as I say, not as I do," than most of us would like to admit. What it is not, is random.

The real question — the one this essay is built around — is not whether the policy is well drafted. It is whether the person holding actual authority is a moral actor. Those are two entirely different questions, and institutions spend almost all their energy on the first while carefully avoiding the second.

If you think your wonderfully moral policy document matters - a lot, a little, or at all - best not read on. The news that follows won't be welcome.

## The Unwritten Rules

Steve Simpson spent a career studying what he calls Unwritten Ground Rules - the actual operating manual of an organisation, as distinct from the laminated one HR hands you on day one.¹ Nobody reads the values poster in the lobby and thinks "ah yes, that's how we really do things here." You learn the real rules by watching. Who gets promoted after a mess. Who gets quietly moved sideways the week after they raised their hand. Who gets protected, and who gets thrown under the bus the moment it becomes convenient. You learn the unwritten rules by how you are punished in the main.

Why does it work this way? Because words like integrity, respect and accountability mean almost nothing on their own. They're Rorschach tests. Everyone nods along in the town hall, because nobody is against integrity in principle. The actual content of the word only shows up when it costs someone something.

A company says it welcomes dissent and challenge. Fine. Now watch what happens to the person who challenges a senior executive in front of their peers. A government department says it values transparency. Fine. Now watch how it treats the whistleblower. A church preaches truth and accountability. Fine. Now watch what happens when one of its own is the one accused. Persecution is what happens in the main.

In every case, behaviour is the interpreter of doctrine. The policy document is the marketing copy. The unwritten rules are the product.

And the unwritten rules win, because they're adaptive - they're the result of thousands of people, independently, learning what's safe and what isn't, what gets rewarded and what gets quietly punished. Most of this learning is unconscious. Nobody sits down and decides to be cynical. They just notice, over time, what actually happens to people who take the brochure literally.

## Built To Read The Room

To understand why this adaptation is so fast and so durable, you have to step outside management theory entirely and into biology.

Robert Sapolsky's *Behave* is the best single synthesis I've read on why humans do what they do, and the uncomfortable headline is this: almost everyone else is playing a game most of us pretend not to notice - social status.² For most of human history, where you sat in the hierarchy was the difference between eating and not eating, breeding and not breeding, living and dying. So we evolved an extraordinarily fine-tuned radar for rank, punishment, inclusion and exclusion. It runs below conscious thought, for some it is intuitive.

Swap the savannah for an open-plan office, a parish, a barracks, or a parliament, and the radar still works exactly the same way. Employees track status. Soldiers track command relationships. Clergy track ecclesiastical favour. Citizens track who can say what about whom without consequence. This is especially true in the age of social media.None of this requires a memo. It requires only that people are paying attention, which they always are, because their ancestors who didn't pay attention didn't have descendants.

So when an organisation's stated values and its actual reward structure point in different directions, you are not asking people to overcome a bad habit. You are asking them to override several hundred thousand years of personal survival focused risk management behaviours. Good luck with that.

## How We Defend What We Believe

There's a second layer on top of the status radar, and it's about belief rather than behaviour.

Leon Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance showed that when people are confronted with evidence that contradicts something they believe deeply, the path of least resistance isn't to update the belief - it's to reinterpret the evidence.³ His earlier study, *When Prophecy Fails*, is the classic illustration: a doomsday cult whose prophecy doesn't arrive doesn't disband in embarrassment, it doubles down and recruits harder.⁶ The failure becomes proof of faith rather than proof of error. If that sounds familiar from corporate life, it should.

> Having a stated policy of high levels of integration, collaboration, team work and co-operative behaviors across an office, then running a hot desk approach when no one can establish patterns of interaction is a prime example of cognitive dissonance.

Ziva Kunda took this further with motivated reasoning - we don't just defend existing beliefs against threats, we actively go looking for evidence that supports the conclusion we'd already prefer, and we apply a much higher evidential bar to anything that doesn't.⁴ Better to believe what you know is nonsense than go through the psychological pain of having your belief ripped off. Its not better, but its the choice that is made.

Run this through an organisation and you get a predictable, repeating pattern. Leaders believe the culture is healthy because nobody is shouting at them in meetings. Employees know the culture isn't healthy, precisely because shouting in meetings is how you end up on a list. Simpson's own surveys found this gap again and again - leaders rate openness, fairness and accountability consistently higher than the people below them do.¹ Neither side is necessarily lying. They're reporting on two different organisations that happen to share an address. The power disparity, punishers and punished explains some of the perception gap.

If you want to see how language itself gets bent to protect a belief system, Amanda Montell's *Cultish* is worth a read - it's about the specific vocabulary cults and high-control groups use to make dissent unthinkable.⁷ You don't need a robe and a compound for this to apply. Plenty of corporate and political language does exactly the same job, just with better lighting. Some fine examples to make George Orwell proud, you can add your own definition of what you think it really means:

- Work at pace.
- Take offline.
- Circle back.
- Radical candour. Never, ever try this fyi.
- Paradigm shift. etc


## Leadership Is The Loudest Policy

The US Army's "Be, Know, Do" leadership doctrine, largely shaped by Boyd "Mac" Harris, made a point that should be obvious and somehow still isn't: leadership is demonstrated, not declared.⁵ Soldiers don't learn the organisation's real values from the field manual. They learn them from watching what their commander actually does when it's inconvenient to do the right thing.

This generalises completely. Nobody outside the senior levels of staff reads the code of conduct closely as if it is true. Everybody watches what happens to the executive who breaks it.

A company can publish a beautiful code of ethics. If the people at the top are quietly exempt from it, the lesson learned by everyone else is that ethics are conditional - and they will calibrate their own behaviour accordingly, because see the previous section on status radar.

A church can preach moral responsibility from the pulpit every week. If favoured clergy get handled differently to everyone else when something goes wrong, the congregation learns that status outranks principle, no matter what's printed in the missal. This, and not the rules or morals, is what drives actual behaviours.

A government can proclaim transparency in its founding charter. If inconvenient truths get "managed" rather than published, the civil service learns that loyalty beats openness, and acts on that lesson long after the charter is forgotten.

Leadership is the translation layer between doctrine and culture. Whatever leaders reward (be it to advance their own interests or the organizations), tolerate, or quietly let slide - that's the actual policy. Everything else is gaslighting.

This is the question the policy document never asks and the organisation never answers honestly: is the person at the top a moral actor? Not by stated values — by demonstrated behaviour when it costs them something. If they are, the culture will reflect it regardless of what the handbook says. If they are not, no policy will constrain them, because they control the consequences, and policy without consequences is just typography. The written code is silent on the only variable that actually matters.

## Power Makes You Deaf

Here's the part that makes all of this self-reinforcing rather than self-correcting.

Power doesn't just give someone more influence. It changes the information that reaches them. As authority goes up, candour goes down. Subordinates get cautious. Bad news gets softened, delayed, or simply doesn't make the trip upstairs at all. The result is that the people with the most power to fix a culture problem are frequently the last to know it exists.

A CEO can sincerely believe people feel safe speaking up, because nobody objects in the room. People aren't objecting in the room because they've correctly worked out that objecting in the room is a career-limiting move. A bishop can believe the reporting process works because complaints rarely escalate. The complainants worked out years ago that escalating gets you nowhere, so they stopped trying. A political leader can believe a policy is popular because criticism of it has become expensive to voice.

This isn't usually malice. It's a feedback loop quietly switching itself off. The leader becomes dependent on a version of reality the organisation itself is curating for them - and the gap between the official story and the lived one widens every year nobody calls it out.

Jennifer Fraser's *Gaslit Brain* is a useful read here, even though it's framed around individual relationships - the dynamic of having your own observations quietly invalidated by someone with more power than you scales up to institutions disturbingly well.⁸

This is also where the gap between policy content and policy utility becomes starkest. The content of a policy — what it says, what it prohibits, what process it mandates — is largely irrelevant if the power disparity between the individual and the institution makes enforcement practically inaccessible. Royal Mail had policies. Ms Jhuti had grounds. What she also had was years of litigation, at personal cost, against an organisation with substantially greater legal resources, that had constructed a procedurally clean process designed to be exactly that — clean on the surface. The Supreme Court eventually found in her favour.¹¹ Most people do not have the stamina, the funds, or the personal circumstances to reach the Supreme Court. The policy said one thing. Power decided another. The legal remedy existed in theory and was almost unreachable in practice. That is not an edge case. That is the normal condition of power disparity in employment.

## When It All Comes Apart

All of this stays comfortably theoretical right up until a powerful person breaks a rule the institution claims is sacred. That's the moment doctrine and culture stop coexisting peacefully and one of them has to lose.

The Catholic Church abuse cases are the starkest version of this. The scandal was never simply that abuse happened - human failure exists in every large institution, full stop. The scandal was that, repeatedly, institutional protection of the institution took priority over protection of victims, in direct contradiction of everything the institution claims to stand for. The unwritten rule - protect the institution - quietly outranked the written one, for decades, across multiple countries and denominations. Why? Because your job and your career relied on you looking the other way. So many, many people did exactly that. Plus the cognitive dissonance aspect, they truly did not want to believe that it was true given the moral disparity. If the church/religion really did that, it could not be moral authority any more. Governments are excellent at this. Pick the famous issues, China, UK, US, Myanmar, etc, etc.

Wells Fargo's fake-accounts scandal is the corporate version of the same story. The official values statement talked about ethics and customer focus. The actual incentive structure paid out for opening accounts customers never asked for, and punished staff who didn't hit the numbers. Employees didn't need to read a memo to know which one was real.

Enron is the one I've written about at length elsewhere on this site, and it fits the same template. The stated values were integrity and respect, framed on the wall in the lobby. The actual culture rewarded short-term reported earnings by any accounting means available, and punished anyone who asked inconvenient questions about cash flow. Adaptive culture won. It always does, once the two are pointed in different directions for long enough.

The same mechanism runs through examples that feel politically different but are analytically identical. Cancel culture — the organised pursuit of reputational destruction in the name of accountability — is the left's own demonstration of the thesis. A movement explicitly organised around holding power to account for stated values has repeatedly proven willing to suppress dissent, exclude internal critics, and deploy social punishment without due process, all while claiming the moral high ground. The stated values of fairness, inclusion and voice are selectively weaponised for an alternate purpose: enforcement of ideological conformity and protection of in-group power. The mechanism is the same as the church protecting its clergy, the corporation protecting its rainmaker, or the army closing ranks around a senior officer. The language is different. The structure is not.

What these cases share — across institution type, ideology, sector and century — is not a common set of values but a common human tendency. Stated values selectively weaponised for an alternate purpose, protecting the powerful at the expense of the principle. The cases differ wildly in scale and subject matter, but the mechanism is identical: behavioural adaptation eventually overwhelms rhetorical commitment, every single time the incentives and the values disagree for long enough.

## Policy Is Not Prevention

Before we get to the question of whether a company can even hold a moral stance, there's a more basic problem with policy as a behavioural tool. It doesn't work.

Consider the death penalty. If you wanted to design the most extreme possible deterrent to a specific behaviour, state-sanctioned execution of the perpetrator is roughly the ceiling. You cannot threaten more - not materially, not legally. The evidence that it actually reduces murder rates is, to put it charitably, weak. The National Research Council's 2012 review looked at three decades of studies and concluded that existing research was fundamentally flawed and should not be used to inform judgements about deterrent effects.¹⁰ Comparison across US states with and without capital punishment doesn't show the pattern you'd expect if the deterrent were working. Criminologists who study this consistently find that what deters is the *certainty* of being caught, not the *severity* of punishment.

That's the upper bound. The maximum possible material disincentive, applied to an act that is unambiguous, visible, and investigated by the state with significant resource. Murder is not subtle. The victim does not remain in the building. The evidence does not depend on someone's subjective account of a private conversation.

Now contrast that with the behaviours this essay is actually about. Misogyny in a performance review. Preferential treatment of someone's protégé. A hiring decision that isn't fraud, isn't illegal, but isn't remotely fair. Sexual harassment that is deniable, ambiguous, and happened behind a closed door. Exclusion from a meeting that matters, attributed to scheduling. A tone. A pattern. None of these are open acts. None leave clean evidence. None generate a police report. The perpetrator in almost every case is more senior than the person harmed, which means they control the narrative, the process, and often the outcome of any complaint.

If the harshest sanction a state can deploy struggles to suppress clear, unambiguous, evidenced behaviour, what exactly is a policy document in an employee handbook going to do about behaviour that is subtle, hidden, and structurally protected by the power of the person doing it?

The power disparity that enables most of these behaviours is not incidental - it is the point. It is not going away. You cannot policy your way out of a hierarchy, and hierarchies will always create the conditions under which the person at the top can do things the person at the bottom cannot. That is not a design flaw. That is what a hierarchy is.

The obvious counter-argument at this point is: fine, policy alone doesn't prevent anything, but better enforcement would. If the certainty of being caught were high enough, even subtle behaviour would be deterred. It's a reasonable theoretical position. The problem is that in the cases where the behaviour is worst, enforcement certainty and power concentration move in opposite directions almost by definition. The more powerful the perpetrator, the lower the probability that the enforcement mechanism ever reaches them — because in most institutions, the enforcement mechanism reports to power.

Consider the BBC. It is an organisation with, in all likelihood, more formal policies, safeguarding frameworks, HR processes, grievance procedures and editorial codes than almost any comparable institution in the country. It did not prevent Jimmy Savile, whose abuse was an open secret for decades and who continued to be platformed and celebrated throughout. It did not prevent the situation involving Huw Edwards, a senior on-screen figure whose conduct became the subject of internal concern before eventually becoming public. It did not prevent allegations involving Russell Brand during his time at the corporation, which were reported in detail by The Sunday Times and Channel 4 in 2023. Three separate cases. One institution. Every conceivable policy on the books. The internal machinery in each case moved to protect the institution and, by extension, the individuals generating value for it — not the people harmed.

The Harrods case is, if anything, more instructive. Following Mohamed Al Fayed's death in 2023, a Channel 4 documentary gathered testimony from numerous former employees alleging serious and repeated sexual abuse. Al Fayed owned the building, controlled HR, ran security, and personally selected the staff he targeted. There was no internal reporting mechanism that was not, in effect, reporting to him. Policy in that context was not just ineffective — it was structurally impossible. The perpetrator and the enforcer were the same person.

In the hedge fund sector, the Financial Times published detailed reporting in June 2023 on allegations against Crispin Odey, founder of Odey Asset Management. The FT documented that complaints had been made over many years and had not resulted in meaningful consequences, with Odey remaining in control of the firm until partner pressure following the FT's publication eventually forced his departure. The reporting, which Odey has disputed, described an environment in which those raising concerns found the machinery turned against them rather than in their favour.

In each of these cases — BBC, Harrods, the FT's Odey reporting — the pattern is not that policy failed to deter. It is that the enforcement mechanism itself was captured. Complaints went to people who depended on the perpetrator, feared them, or had their own reasons not to act. The internal machinery supported the perpetrator. This is not a failure of policy design. It is power working exactly as it does.

The instructive contrast is the legal stick. What materially advanced women's rights was not the moral claim alone — the moral claim had existed for a long time without producing commensurate change. It was the law. The Sex Discrimination Act, the Equal Pay Act, employment tribunal rights, and the legal infrastructure that followed gave women an external route to pursue cases that sat entirely outside the institution accused. An independent forum, an independent arbiter, a remedy enforceable by the state. The company was no longer judge and jury in its own cause. That structural separation is what made the difference. Not the moral argument, which was already well made. The coercive external mechanism that did not depend on the goodwill of the institution to function.

Inside companies, no equivalent exists. The grievance procedure reports to HR, which reports to management, which has every institutional incentive not to air the organisation's dirty laundry publicly, validate claims that create liability, or produce findings that embarrass senior people. The company investigates itself, judges itself, and decides its own sentence. In any other context we would regard this as an obvious conflict of interest. In employment, we have normalised it. The result is that internal policy operates in a closed system where the institution controls both the process and the outcome — precisely the conditions under which the cases above were allowed to continue for as long as they did.

*Jhuti v Royal Mail Group* [2019] UKSC 55 is the case that lays this bare most cleanly. Ms Jhuti raised concerns early in her employment that a colleague was breaching Ofcom guidance and that bonuses were being mis-awarded. Her line manager's response was to make clear that pursuing those concerns would jeopardise her position. She withdrew the allegations. The manager then constructed a capability case against her — manufactured targets, mandatory weekly one-to-ones applied to her alone, sustained pressure — and she was dismissed on capability grounds by a separate dismissing officer who had no knowledge of the original disclosures. The process looked clean. A legitimate procedure had been followed by someone with no apparent motive. Royal Mail had built a firewall between the real reason and the recorded reason.

It took the Supreme Court to pierce it. The judgment held that where someone in the hierarchy above the employee determines that they should be dismissed for whistleblowing but conceals it behind an invented reason that a decision-maker then adopts, the real reason for dismissal is the hidden one. The Court said it was necessary to "penetrate through the invention." The compensation, after a lengthy legal battle, was £2.3 million.

The case is instructive not because the outcome was wrong — ultimately it was corrected — but because of what was required to correct it. Not the internal grievance procedure. Not HR. Not management. The Supreme Court of the United Kingdom, reached after years of litigation funded by the individual, against an organisation with substantially greater resources. That is the actual cost of the enforcement route available to someone whose employer investigates itself and finds in its own favour. The legal stick exists. The handle is a long way from most people's reach.

The honest answer is that policy's real and primary value is post-hoc. It is curative, not preventative. It provides the language to name a violation, the framework to investigate it, the grounds to dismiss or prosecute, and the institutional cover to act without appearing arbitrary. Without a stated policy, you cannot credibly sanction anyone for anything, because there is no prior standard they can be held to have breached. That is what policy actually does. That is why it exists.

Before an enforcement event, what policy produces is not substantive behavioural change. It produces performative adherence. People signal compliance. They attend the training, sign the acknowledgement form, nod through the all-hands. The unwritten rules remain exactly what they were. The status radar is still running. The person who knows which behaviour gets rewarded and which gets punished has not updated their model. They have simply learned to perform the values publicly while continuing to act on the actual incentive structure privately. Performative adherence is not nothing - it creates a shared vocabulary and sets visible norms that make post-hoc enforcement easier to justify. But it is not the same thing as substantive influence on what people actually do.

This is the argument no policy document ever makes. Read any code of conduct, any safeguarding framework, any anti-harassment policy, and you will find it presented as preventative. "We do not tolerate." "This organisation is committed to." "All staff are required to." The language is prospective. The implicit claim is that the existence of the policy changes what happens. That the act of writing it down reduces the incidence of the behaviour it prohibits.

It doesn't. Or at least, not in any meaningful proportion to the institutional effort invested in producing it.

The actual function - post-hoc correction; liability coverage; the creation of enforceable standards - is never stated plainly, because stating it plainly would be damaging. "This policy will not prevent misconduct, but it will give us grounds to act after the fact and protect us from vicarious liability" is a truthful summary of what most policies actually achieve. No institution publishes that sentence. Instead they publish the preventative fiction, because the preventative fiction is what maintains legitimacy, satisfies regulators, and reassures the public that the institution takes these matters seriously.

Which brings us back to the BBC. Huw Edwards, Russell Brand, Jimmy Savile: three cases, one institution, every conceivable policy instrument in place. The policies did not prevent. They were not designed to prevent, whatever language they used. Their actual function was post-hoc - and as the cases above illustrate, even that function fails when the enforcement machinery is controlled by those with the most to lose from using it. In the most serious cases, you end up with neither prevention nor cure. Just the policy, sitting on a shelf, doing its only reliable job: allowing the institution to say, when it finally becomes indefensible not to act, that a standard existed all along.

## Can A Company Even Have A Moral Stance?

Here's the question underneath all of this that rarely gets asked directly: can a legal entity have a moral stance at all, or is morality exclusively a property of individual human beings?

Milton Friedman's famous 1970 New York Times piece went straight at this.⁹ His argument was that a corporation is a legal fiction - a construct. It can sign contracts, be sued, hold assets. What it cannot do is have a conscience. Only people have moral responsibilities. When an executive deploys corporate resources on "social purposes" - a values statement, a charitable programme, a politically motivated stance - they are spending money that belongs to shareholders without their consent. In Friedman's framing, this is a form of unilateral taxation. The executive is deciding what's morally important with other people's money, and calling it corporate virtue.

It's an uncomfortable argument precisely because it holds up. The company itself has no interior life. It doesn't believe anything. It doesn't feel anything. The moral character of an institution is entirely composed of the moral choices made by the people inside it - particularly the people at the top, given everything in the sections above. A company that "stands for" something is really a group of executives who have decided to stand for it, often at shareholder expense, often in ways that the unwritten rules will quietly make optional for anyone who'd prefer not to.

This has some sharper edges now than it did in 1970. Corporations in most jurisdictions have legal personhood to varying degrees - they can be criminally prosecuted, fined, even effectively "executed" by regulators who revoke their licence to operate. If we're willing to treat a company as a moral actor in court, it feels slightly inconsistent to say it can't have moral obligations the rest of the time. The Citizens United ruling in the US extended First Amendment speech protections to corporations, which is its own rabbit hole.

The ESG movement is the current attempt to resolve this by saying that long-term shareholder value and social responsibility are the same thing - that the company "benefits" from having good values in the same way a person does, through reputation, employee retention, regulatory goodwill, and so on. This is clever but somewhat circular. It just converts the moral question into a financial one, which is arguably where Friedman started.

What this question does usefully is complete the essay's central argument. If moral agency lives only in people, then a corporation is by definition an amoral actor — a legal construct with no conscience, no values, and no capacity for self-regulation. Expecting it to police its own culture is equivalent to expecting a machine to feel guilty. The machine does what its operators instruct. The institution does what its actual authority — the people at the top — incentivises, tolerates, and models.

No amoral actor self-regulates. This is not a cynical observation. It is a structural one. An entity without moral agency cannot be moved by moral argument, values statements, or codes of conduct. It can only be moved by external coercion — regulatory sanction, legal liability, public exposure — or by the moral character of the individuals who run it. Which brings us back, inevitably, to the top of the org chart, and to the question the policy document never asks.

## Conclusion

Organisations tend to assume culture is something you build with policies, values statements, training modules and a slide deck from HR. These things aren't worthless, but they are nowhere near sufficient, because people don't learn culture by being told. They learn it by watching.

> People are policy. Culture disseminates from the top down.

Put the pieces together and you get a fairly unflattering, but consistent, picture. Simpson's Unwritten Ground Rules explain how people work out the real rules from observed consequences rather than the official ones.¹ Sapolsky explains why we're built to notice status, risk and punishment faster than almost anything else.² Festinger and Kunda explain how both sides of an organisation construct and defend entirely different versions of reality, neither one dishonestly.³ ⁴ Mac Harris reminds us that leadership is communicated through example, not proclamation.⁵

> Professed doctrine is how an institution wants to be seen.
> Adaptive culture is what its people have actually learned they need to do to get on.

Where the two line up, you get trust and resilience. Where they don't, the adaptive culture wins - not eventually, immediately, the moment anyone is paying close enough attention.

> When policy and behaviour don't align, it is likely the personal traits of the senior person that sets the scene. If the person with the power runs against the policy, the policy framework is entirely redundant. If the person is aligned, then you don't need the policy at all.

The central question, then, is not whether the policy is well drafted. It is whether the person with actual authority is a moral actor. Stated moral authority — the values statement, the code of conduct, the published commitments — belongs to the institution. Actual authority belongs to whoever controls the consequences: promotions, dismissals, budgets, narratives, and the framing of reality that reaches the board. Where those two things are held by the same person and that person is a moral actor, the policy is superfluous. Where they are not, the policy is impotent.

No amoral actor self-regulates. No internal process designed by an institution and controlled by the same power structure can compel them to. The only instrument that has historically moved behaviour in the face of amoral authority is external coercion — the law, the regulator, the press, the court. And as Jhuti illustrates, even the law requires resources, stamina, and years that most people do not have.

Policy's genuine value is therefore post-hoc: it names violations, enables sanctions, protects the institution legally, and makes accountability arguable after the fact. Before an enforcement event, it produces performative adherence at best — the public signalling of values without the private internalisation of them. This is not a design flaw that better policy could fix. It is the inherent limit of any written instrument applied to behaviour driven by status, survival, and the reading of power.

And yet no policy is ever presented this way. They are all held out as preventative. The language is always prospective — we do not tolerate, we are committed to, all staff are required to — because the preventative fiction is more useful than the curative truth. It satisfies regulators. It reassures the public. It allows the institution, when scandal eventually forces its hand, to point to a document that existed all along and say the standard was always there.

It was. It just wasn't doing what anyone claimed it was doing. And in the cases that matter most — where the actual authority is held by someone with no moral inclination to constrain themselves — it never could have.

Achieving anything worthwhile requires acting in concert. But there's not a fine line between coordinated cooperation and controlled obedience - there's a wide gap, and most rigid organisations have fallen into it without noticing. Duress gets you obedience, never innovation or progress. Progress needs experimentation, and therefore failure, and amoral authority cannot tolerate failure any more than it can tolerate dissent. That's why these regimes are regressive almost by definition.

Personally, I'd rather work as part of a coalition of the willing. Watch how people are treated, not what the poster on the wall says. Look for excuses, not reasons. The moral character of the person at the top is the only policy that counts.

## Key Takeaways

- The central question is not whether the policy is well drafted. It is whether the person with actual authority is a moral actor. If they are, the policy is superfluous. If they are not, the policy is impotent.
- No amoral actor self-regulates. No internal process controlled by the same power structure can compel them to. Only external coercion — law, regulator, press — has historically moved behaviour in the face of amoral authority.
- Stated moral authority belongs to the institution. Actual authority belongs to whoever controls consequences. Where these diverge, actual authority wins without exception.
- Policy's real value is post-hoc and curative: it names violations and enables sanctions after the fact. Before an enforcement event it produces only performative adherence. No policy ever states this, because the preventative fiction is institutionally more useful than the curative truth.
- The enforcement mechanism in most institutions reports to the same power it is meant to constrain. The company investigates itself, judges itself, and decides its own sentence. Jhuti required the Supreme Court to correct what HR could not.
- Stated values selectively weaponised for an alternate purpose — protecting the powerful at the expense of the principle — is the common mechanism across every case in this essay, regardless of institution type, ideology, or century.
- The moral character of the person at the top is the only policy that counts. Everything else is laminated.
