09 · Key Findings
The price gap is structural, not cyclical. UK industrial electricity prices were broadly in line with peers until ~2010. Since then they have diverged dramatically. By 2023 the UK pays 3.3× the US rate, 46% above the IEA median. Gas prices do not explain this — UK industrial gas prices are near the European median. The premium is in the electricity-to-gas price ratio: ~6× in the UK vs 2.5× in France and 3× in Germany.
Bilateral annual correlation is statistically weak. Contemporaneous Pearson correlation between annual electricity price changes and GDP growth is not significant for most G20 countries. South Korea is the exception (r=−0.53, p=0.008). The effect operates over structural timescales — investment relocation and deindustrialisation over 5–20 years — not year-to-year.
Two-period structural comparison is suggestive. The country with the smallest electricity price increase (USA, +29%) showed the largest GDP growth improvement. The country with the largest price increase (UK, +127%) showed the largest growth deceleration. Burke & Mizrahi (2022) estimate a 10% energy price increase reduces OECD growth by ~0.15pp on average; the UK magnitudes are consistent.
UK manufacturing share halved. From 15.5% (2000) to 9.6% (2022), a 38% decline. Germany held above 22% throughout. Energy-intensive industries — steel, ceramics, chemicals, glass — are where high electricity prices bite hardest. The Stellantis Luton closure (2024) cited energy cost competitiveness explicitly.
UK electricity generation has fallen 26% from its 2005 peak. Total generation peaked at 398 TWh in 2005 and had fallen to 293 TWh by 2023 — a decline of 26.4%. Per capita generation peaked at 6,525 kWh/person in 2005 and fell to 4,315 kWh/person in 2023, a 33.9% decline. Population growth amplifies the per capita effect. The decline reflects improved efficiency, deindustrialisation, and some demand destruction — but in a growing economy, sustained generation decline is unusual and warrants scrutiny.
The UK is generating less electricity per person than it did 30 years ago. Per capita generation in 2023 (4,315 kWh) is below the 1993 level (5,569 kWh). This reversal is structurally significant. The UK has added 10 million people since 1993 while generating less total electricity. This is partly efficiency gains in buildings and appliances — but it also reflects the hollowing out of energy-intensive industrial production. The Wikipedia figure of 3,239 kWh/person average use (2023) is consumption rather than generation; generation includes transmission losses and industrial consumption not captured in household statistics.
