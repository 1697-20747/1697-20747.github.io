Correlation vs. Time Horizon — The Central Finding

The Longer the Horizon, the Stronger the Signal

Year-on-year: r = 0.21 (r² = 0.04). Annual changes in GDP explain almost none of the annual volatility in stock market capitalisation. Markets are driven by sentiment, interest rates, earnings revisions, and risk appetite on short horizons.

Decade: Median r = 0.88. The relationship is strong in most decades but breaks down in inflationary/stagflationary periods (1970s, r = 0.50) and after asset-bubble bursts (2000s, r = 0.47).

20-year: r consistently 0.87–0.98. Cyclical noise averages out; the structural link between economic output and aggregate corporate wealth becomes the dominant force.

50-year: r = 0.96–0.99. Over half-century spans, the relationship is near-deterministic. The power-law fit (Market Cap ≈ e⁻¹·⁹⁵ × GDP¹·²¹) holds with remarkable stability.

Implication: The Buffett Indicator is a poor timing tool but a powerful long-run valuation anchor. Extreme deviations (e.g. 2021: 229%) tend to mean-revert, but the reversion can take a decade.

Statistical Summary & Conclusions

Does Correlation Rise with Time Interval? — Yes, Decisively

Short run (1 year): The Buffett Indicator is essentially uncorrelated with future GDP growth on a year-on-year basis (r = 0.21). Markets are forward-looking and dominated by sentiment, discount rates, and earnings expectations. GDP is a lagging measure of economic output. They simply operate on different timescales.

Medium run (decade): Correlation rises sharply to a median of ~0.88, but with notable exceptions. The stagflationary 1970s (r = 0.50) and the post-dot-com bust 2000s (r = 0.47) show that structural regime shifts — where inflation decouples real GDP from nominal equity values — can suppress the relationship for an entire decade.

Long run (20–50 years): Correlation stabilises at 0.95–0.99. Over these spans, business cycles, recessions, bubbles, and geopolitical shocks are noise; the underlying growth of corporate earnings power tracks the growth of the broader economy.

Power law: The best-fit model is log(Mkt Cap) = −1.95 + 1.21 × log(GDP). The exponent β = 1.21 > 1 means markets have systematically grown faster than GDP over 95 years — consistent with rising profit margins, globalisation of US corporations, and increasing financial inclusion. At current levels (200%), the ratio stands 67 percentage points above its long-run regression prediction of ~133%.

Verdict for investors: The Buffett Indicator is a long-run thermometer, not a short-run timer. At 200%, it signals that a decade of below-average returns is the statistically likely outcome — not necessarily an imminent crash, but a structural headwind for passive equity portfolios over the next 10–20 years.
