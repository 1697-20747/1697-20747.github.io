Feynman On Understanding

“What I cannot create, I do not understand.” — Richard Feynman, Nobel Laureate in Physics (1965), found written on his blackboard at the time of his death in 1988. Feynman received the prize for his work developing quantum electrodynamics.

Billion Here

“A billion here, a billion there, and pretty soon you’re talking real money.” — popularly attributed to US Senator Everett Dirksen. No record of him actually saying it survives in his papers or recorded speeches; quote researchers regard it as apocryphal. Still the canonical line for how casually large numbers get thrown around budgets.

Disease Of Dogs

“Gratitude is a disease of dogs.” — attributed to Joseph Stalin, via Milovan Djilas’s memoir Conversations with Stalin. Not independently verified beyond Djilas’s account, but consistent with the source.

Fall To Training

“You don’t rise to the level of your expectations, you fall to the level of your training.” — popularly attributed to the ancient Greek poet Archilochus. Near-certainly a modern saying with no ancient source; it circulates in military and Navy SEAL training culture long before anyone traced it to a 7th-century-BC poet. In a competition, you don’t rise to the level of your competition — you fall to the level of your training, whoever first said so.

Lotus In The Mud

The lotus blossoms in the mud, untainted; it interacts with the world yet remains unstained. A recurring metaphor across Buddhist scripture (the Buddha compares himself to a lotus, born in the water and rising above it unsoiled, in the Anguttara Nikaya) rather than a single traceable sutra line — the image, not a specific verse, is the point.

Men Make Their Own History

“Men make their own history, but they do not make it just as they please; they do not make it under circumstances chosen by themselves, but under circumstances directly encountered, given and transmitted from the past.” — Karl Marx, The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852).

Never Too Late Eliot

“It’s never too late to be who you ought to have been.” — commonly attributed to George Eliot. Except she probably never wrote it. Quote Investigator traces this one to greeting cards and inspirational-poster circulation, not to Middlemarch or anything else in Eliot’s actual output. Doesn’t make it bad advice, though.

One Way Rifle Range

No one gets a medal of valour from time on the one way rifle range. Best not be a tyrant, as one day you will end up on the two way range, and your subordinates will very likely aim with intent. As they should.

Ran Out Of Stones

“The Stone Age didn’t end because we ran out of stones.” — commonly attributed to Sheikh Ahmed Zaki Yamani, former Saudi oil minister. Widely repeated, thinly sourced — energy-policy figures from Yamani to Steven Chu get credited with versions of this line. Good line regardless of who coined it first.

Thought Spoken Is A Lie

“A thought once uttered is a lie.” — Fyodor Tyutchev, from the poem “Silentium!” (1830). Usually passed around as an anonymous “Russian proverb” — it isn’t, it’s a specific poet’s line, and a much better one for knowing its source.
