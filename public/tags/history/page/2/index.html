The Royal Charter of the Bank of England (1694)

The Bank of England was constituted by Royal Charter granted by William III in 1694. The Charter emerged from a straightforward transaction — a syndicate of merchants led by William Paterson agreed to lend £1.2 million to a cash-strapped Crown at 8% interest, and in return received the right to incorporate as a bank with the power to issue notes, hold deposits, and extend credit. What made it revolutionary was not the banking itself but the constitutional arrangement underneath it — the loan was permanent, converting a one-off debt into an enduring obligation of the state. Parliament guaranteed repayment, binding future governments to the commitments of the present one. This created the world’s first funded national debt and established the principle that sovereign borrowing could extend across generations. The Charter is the founding document of modern public finance as much as it is of central banking. ...

Lombard Street

The foundational text on central banking. Written in 1873, Bagehot explains how the Bank of England should act as lender of last resort during financial panics — lend freely, at high rates, against good collateral. Every financial crisis since has been measured against whether policymakers followed his prescription. Observations The classic must-read if you ever study central banking. Or work in one. Go on, give it a go.

A Concise History of Warfare

Montgomery’s own account of warfare from ancient times to the mid-twentieth century, written with the authority of a man who commanded armies across North Africa and Northwest Europe. Clear-eyed on strategy, leadership, and the recurring mistakes that commanders make across centuries. Observations A nice summary of key battles, with a good tactical vs strategy examination. Key observation in literal sense from this book is that tactics, strategy and resources need to be in alignment to attain success. Everyone who ever worked on a large project should read this as many times as it takes to be understood. You cannot escape the constraint of resources. How many times in your life have you seen things fail because the resources — people, capital, time, technology — were materially diminished compared to the ask? Failure in such a situation is the fault of the person setting the strategy, not that of those executing. Strategy at the end of the day is the articulation of the bridge from means to the end. It reflects the painful, but necessary, choices made in the face of constraint. There are always constraints.
